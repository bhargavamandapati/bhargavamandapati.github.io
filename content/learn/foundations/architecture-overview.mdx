Almost every AAOS bug you will ever debug comes down to one question: **which
layer is lying to you?**

A number on the screen is wrong. Is the sensor wrong? Is the car's own computer
sending the wrong value? Is Android misreading it? Is the app displaying it
badly? Until you can name the layers, you cannot answer that, and you end up
guessing.

So this page is the map. Take your time with it — everything else in the
curriculum refers back to it.

## First, the shape of the problem

A car contains dozens of small computers called <T>ECU</T>s. One controls the
engine, another the doors, another the climate system. They were designed decades
before Android existed and they know nothing about it.

Android needs to show the driver what those computers know, and sometimes tell
them to do something. But Android cannot simply "read the car" — there is no such
thing. It has to go through several translation steps.

The stack is those translation steps, stacked up.

<Plain>
Seven layers, but they group into three ideas:

1. **The top three** are Android as you already know it — apps and the framework.
2. **The middle two** are the car-specific part of Android: Car Service and the
   Vehicle HAL.
3. **The bottom two** are the car itself, which is not Android at all.

The dashed line is the boundary between "Google's code" and "the car maker's
code". Most integration problems happen right around it.
</Plain>

## Walking down the layers

### Layer 1 — Automotive apps

These are ordinary Android apps, with two extra rules.

First, they cannot touch the vehicle directly. There is no way for an app to open
the car's wiring and read a message. It must ask the platform.

Second, they must obey the driver distraction rules covered later in the
curriculum — limits on what may be displayed while the car is moving.

<Plain>
If you write an app for a car, you are on this layer. You do not modify anything
below it; you use it.
</Plain>

### Layer 2 — The Car API (`android.car`)

This is a library your app links against. It is the *public* face of everything
car-related.

You already know this pattern from ordinary Android: to play a sound you use
`android.media`; to send a notification you use `android.app`. To read the cabin
temperature you use <T>android.car</T>.

The two names worth knowing here:

- `VehiclePropertyIds` — the catalogue of every piece of vehicle data Android
  defines, as a long list of constants.
- <T>CarPropertyManager</T> — the object you actually call to read or watch a
  value.

<Analogy title="Like a menu and a waiter">
`VehiclePropertyIds` is the menu: it lists everything that can be ordered.
`CarPropertyManager` is the waiter: you tell them what you want and they go and
get it. Neither of them cooks anything — the kitchen is several layers down.
</Analogy>

### Layer 3 — The Android framework

The ordinary Android you already know: activity management, window management,
the package manager. AAOS does not replace this. It adds to it.

### Layer 4 — Car Service

Now we are into the genuinely automotive part.

<T>Car Service</T> is a single process — `com.android.car` — that holds about a
dozen smaller services inside it. It is the centre of gravity of the whole
platform.

| Inside Car Service | What it owns |
| --- | --- |
| `CarPropertyService` | All vehicle data, and who is allowed to see it |
| `CarAudioService` | Which speakers a sound comes out of |
| `CarPowerManagementService` | What happens when the car is switched off |
| `CarUserService` | Driver profiles and switching between them |
| `CarUxRestrictionsManagerService` | What the screen may show while moving |
| `CarWatchdogService` | Noticing when something has stopped responding |

<Plain>
When your app calls `CarPropertyManager.getFloatProperty(...)`, that call travels
into Car Service. Car Service checks whether your app is allowed, then asks the
layer below. It is the gatekeeper for everything vehicle-related.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="Why one command is worth learning first">
A colleague says the cabin temperature reads 0 °C on the test vehicle, in July.

Before opening any code, run:

```bash
adb shell dumpsys car_service --list-properties
```

This asks Car Service: *what vehicle data do you know about?* If
`HVAC_TEMPERATURE_CURRENT` is not in that list, the problem is not in the app,
not in Car Service, and not in the API. The layer below never told Android the
property exists — so no amount of app debugging will help.

That single command routinely saves an afternoon, and it works because Car
Service sits at exactly the right point in the stack to answer the question.
</Scenario>

### Layer 5 — The Vehicle HAL

The <T>VHAL</T> is the narrowest point in the entire stack, and deliberately so.

It offers exactly one thing: a list of named values — <T>vehicle property</T> —
each of which can be read, written, or watched for changes. That is the whole
interface.

<Analogy title="Like a hotel reception desk">
Guests do not wander into the kitchen, the laundry or the boiler room. They ask
at reception, and reception knows how to reach each department.

The VHAL is that desk. Android never talks to the engine computer or the door
controller. It asks the VHAL, and the VHAL knows how to reach them.

The reason hotels work this way is the same reason cars do: it means the
departments can be reorganised without teaching every guest a new route.
</Analogy>

<Plain>
Why so narrow? Because this interface has to stay stable for fifteen years while
everything on both sides of it changes. A small, simple interface is easier to
keep unchanged than a rich one.

Every time the property model feels limiting, that is the reason.
</Plain>

### Layer 6 — The vendor vehicle network service

This layer is **not** in AOSP. It is written by the car maker or their supplier,
and it is different on every vehicle.

Its job is to actually talk to the car: open the <T>CAN</T> connection, read
messages, and convert raw numbers into the units the VHAL promised.

<Scenario title="The classic scaling bug">
A door sensor broadcasts the value `215` on the vehicle network. That is not 215
of anything — the ECU's documentation says the real temperature is
`value × 0.1 − 40`, so `215` means `−18.5 °C`.

If this layer forgets to apply that conversion, Android faithfully reports
`215 °C`. Nothing crashes. No error appears anywhere. The UI displays a number
that is confidently, precisely wrong.

This is why "wrong but stable" values are almost always a translation problem in
this layer, and why the first question is always "what does the raw bus value
say?"
</Scenario>

### Layer 7 — The buses and ECUs

The car's own electronics. Dozens to hundreds of small computers, wired together
with <T>CAN</T> and other networks, running software that is not Android and was
often written before Android existed.

## The dashed line: Treble

The dashed line in the diagram is the most important architectural fact in AAOS,
and it has a name: <T>Treble</T>.

Android is split into separate storage areas called partitions:

- **`/system`** holds the Android framework. Google and the car maker's platform
  team update this.
- **`/vendor`** holds hardware-specific code — including the VHAL. The supplier
  writes this, and it is often frozen for years.

They are only allowed to talk to each other through **fixed, versioned
interfaces**. Not by sharing code. Not by sharing data structures. Not by one
calling a function in the other.

<Analogy title="Like a plug and a socket">
You can replace a lamp without rewiring the house. You can rewire the house
without buying new lamps. That only works because the plug shape is agreed and
does not change.

Treble is that agreement. `/system` is the house wiring; `/vendor` is the lamp.
</Analogy>

<Plain>
**Why this exists:** a car is sold and then driven for fifteen years. Android
releases a new version every year. Without this separation, updating Android
would mean every supplier rewriting and re-testing everything they delivered —
which in practice means the update never happens, and vehicles stay on old,
unpatched software.

Treble is what makes it possible to ship a security fix to a seven-year-old car.
Every restriction it imposes is buying that.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="A conversation you will have">
Someone asks: *"Can you just add a field to that struct so the HAL can pass us
one more value?"*

The answer is no, and the reason is not bureaucracy. That struct is part of a
versioned interface with a binary compatibility guarantee. Adding a field means
publishing a new version of the interface, supporting both in parallel, and
updating the manifest that declares which one the device provides.

It is a scheduled piece of work, not a five-minute change. Knowing that early
prevents a lot of disappointment later.
</Scenario>

## Following one value all the way down

Here is the whole stack in one worked example. Your app wants to display the
cabin temperature.

```kotlin title="What your app writes"
val car = Car.createCar(context)
val propertyManager = car.getCarManager(Car.PROPERTY_SERVICE) as CarPropertyManager

val temperature = propertyManager.getFloatProperty(
    VehiclePropertyIds.HVAC_TEMPERATURE_CURRENT,   // which value
    VehicleAreaSeat.SEAT_ROW_1_LEFT,               // for which seat
)
```

Three lines. Here is what actually happens:

**1. Your app asks the Car API.** `getFloatProperty` is a normal method call
inside your own process. Nothing has left your app yet.

**2. The request crosses to Car Service.** This is <T>Binder</T> — Android's way
for one process to ask another process to do something. Your app pauses while it
waits for an answer.

**3. Car Service checks permission.** Does this app hold
`android.car.permission.CONTROL_CAR_CLIMATE`? If not, it stops here and your app
receives a `SecurityException`.

**4. Car Service asks the VHAL.** "Give me property
`HVAC_TEMPERATURE_CURRENT` for area `SEAT_ROW_1_LEFT`."

**5. The VHAL answers from its cache.** It does not go and ask the car right now
— its own background thread has been listening to the vehicle network and
keeping a recent value. It hands back what it last heard.

**6. The value travels back up** through the same path, and `getFloatProperty`
returns `21.5f`.

<Plain>
Six places for that number to be wrong. When it is wrong, walk them in order
rather than guessing:

1. Is the app asking for the right property and the right seat?
2. Does the app hold the permission? (`SecurityException` if not)
3. Does Car Service know the property exists? (`--list-properties`)
4. Is the VHAL running at all? (`adb shell lshal | grep vehicle`)
5. Is the VHAL's cached value correct? (`--get-property`)
6. Is the raw value from the car being converted correctly?

Steps 3, 4 and 5 are single commands. Most engineers start at step 1, spend two
hours there, and the answer was step 3.
</Plain>

## Layers you are allowed to change

Worth knowing early, because it shapes what job you are actually doing:

| Layer | Can you change it? |
| --- | --- |
| Apps | Yes — this is ordinary app development |
| Car API | Only if you work on the platform |
| Framework | Only if you work on the platform, and it risks CTS failures |
| Car Service | Yes, if you are the car maker — but it is an AOSP fork to maintain |
| Vehicle HAL | Yes — this is the supplier's code |
| Vendor network service | Yes — this is entirely the supplier's |
| ECUs | A different team, often a different company |

<Recap>
- The stack exists because a car's computers and Android speak different
  languages. Each layer is a translation step.
- **Car Service** is the gatekeeper: everything vehicle-related passes through
  it, and it checks permissions.
- The **VHAL** is deliberately narrow — one list of named values — so it can stay
  unchanged for fifteen years.
- **Treble** is the hard boundary between Android's code and the car maker's
  code. It exists so Android can be updated on old vehicles.
- When a value is wrong, **name the layer before guessing.**
  `dumpsys car_service --list-properties` and `lshal | grep vehicle` answer more
  questions faster than reading code.
</Recap>
