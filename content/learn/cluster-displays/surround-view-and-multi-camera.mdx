`evs-rear-view-camera` covered why a single mandated rear-view feed has to
bypass the framework entirely to hit its boot-time deadline. Surround view —
a synthetic top-down image built from four or more cameras — starts from
that same EVS foundation and adds a genuinely different problem on top: the
four feeds are not shown side by side, they are combined into one image that
never existed as a single camera's output.

<Plain>
A rear-view camera shows you what one lens sees, undistorted. A surround-view
system shows you a virtual bird's-eye view of the vehicle from directly
above — an image no physical camera ever captured. It is built by warping
four wide-angle images to a shared ground-plane coordinate system and
blending them together where their fields of view overlap.
</Plain>

## Where the four feeds come from, and why they are wide-angle

Surround view typically uses four cameras — front, rear, and one in each
door mirror or fender — each with a wide (often 180°+) fisheye lens, chosen
specifically so that four cameras can cover the full 360° around the vehicle
with generous overlap at the corners. Each feed still enters the platform
through the same `IEvsCamera` HAL interface `evs-rear-view-camera` already
covered; surround view is a consumer of multiple simultaneous EVS streams,
not a different capture path.

## The stitching pipeline

```text title="From four raw feeds to one synthetic image"
front camera ─┐
rear camera ──┤
left camera ──┼─► per-camera lens undistortion (fisheye correction)
right camera ─┘        │
                        ▼
              perspective warp onto a shared ground-plane mesh
              (calibrated per-vehicle: camera position, angle, lens model)
                        │
                        ▼
              blend overlapping regions (where two cameras see
              the same patch of ground, from different angles)
                        │
                        ▼
              composite top-down image → displayed, same EVS
              boot-time and handover rules as the single-camera case
```

<Callout type="warning" title="Enabling a service type doesn't confirm the camera works">
Wiring up an additional camera service type (surround view, front view, and
similar) through a shell command such as `enable-camera-service-type` is
bookkeeping-only — it records the service-type-to-camera-id mapping and
reports success without ever opening the camera device to check it exists.
The real failure, an `ERROR_UNAVAILABLE` result, only surfaces later when an
app actually calls `startVideoStream()` for that service type. Treat a
shell "successfully enabled" message as unverified until something actually
streams from it.
</Callout>

The blend step is where most of the visible quality problems live. Two
cameras seeing the same patch of ground from different angles will disagree
about anything that has *height* — a curb, a pole, another vehicle's
bumper — because the ground-plane assumption the warp is built on is only
correct for things that are actually flat on the ground. An object with
height appears doubled, smeared, or cut off exactly at the seam between two
cameras' contributions, an artefact inherent to the technique rather than a
tuning mistake.

<Analogy title="Four photographs taped into one map">
Four aerial photographs of the same intersection, each taken from directly
above four different corners and taped together where their edges line up
on the *road surface*, will match perfectly for the road markings and
disagree wherever something tall — a lamppost, a tree — appears in more than
one photo from a different angle, because the tape job only accounts for
things at ground height. Surround-view stitching has exactly the same
inherent seam.
</Analogy>

## Calibration is per-vehicle, not per-model

Every camera's exact position, tilt, and lens distortion is measured and
baked into the warp mesh at the factory or a service centre, because
manufacturing tolerances in where a camera physically sits are large enough,
relative to how close the vehicle is to the ground, to visibly misalign the
seams if two vehicles of the same model use the same generic calibration.
This calibration data typically lives with the vehicle, not the software
build — a windshield or bumper replacement that moves a camera by even a few
millimetres can require recalibration, which is a service-centre workflow
the platform has to support, not just a factory step.

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A fleet reports that surround view "looks fine" on vehicles straight from
the factory and develops a visible seam misalignment on vehicles that have
had front-bumper collision repairs. The camera itself is undamaged and
reports a healthy feed; the repair simply reseated the bumper a few
millimetres differently than the factory calibration assumed, and nothing in
the repair workflow triggered a recalibration. The fix is a service
procedure, not a software one — but the platform still has to expose a way
to trigger recalibration and to detect an obviously wrong result (a check
pattern parked in front of the vehicle, imaged and compared against
expected geometry), because there is no way for software alone to know a
camera has been physically nudged.
</Scenario>

<Callout type="warning" title="The same boot-time budget applies, multiplied">
Surround view inherits every constraint `evs-rear-view-camera` already
described — bypass the framework, hit the deadline, hand over to Android
cleanly — except now four camera streams and a real-time stitching pipeline
all have to be ready inside the same window that used to budget for one.
Treat surround view's timing budget as a harder version of the same
problem, not a separate one.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- Surround view builds a synthetic top-down image from multiple EVS camera
  feeds warped onto a shared ground-plane mesh and blended at their overlaps
  — it reuses the same `IEvsCamera` HAL as the single rear-view case.
- Objects with height distort or double at the seam between two cameras,
  because the ground-plane warp assumption only holds for things that are
  actually flat — this is inherent to the technique, not a defect to tune
  away.
- Calibration is measured per physical vehicle, not shared per model, and a
  body repair can invalidate it without damaging the camera itself.
- The same boot-time-deadline discipline from single-camera EVS applies,
  now across four simultaneous feeds and a real-time stitching pipeline.
</Recap>
