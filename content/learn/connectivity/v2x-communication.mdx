Bluetooth, cellular, and projection all connect the vehicle to something a
human is holding or something sitting in a data centre. **V2X** connects it
to something with no human in the loop at all — another vehicle, a traffic
signal, a road-hazard sensor — and it exists specifically because a cellular
round trip through a server is far too slow for a warning that has to arrive
before two cars collide.

<Plain>
"V2X" is an umbrella term for a vehicle communicating directly with anything
around it: **V2V** (vehicle-to-vehicle), **V2I** (vehicle-to-infrastructure,
like a traffic signal), and **V2P** (vehicle-to-pedestrian, usually via a
phone). The common thread is not the recipient, it is the transport: a
direct, short-range radio link that does not depend on cellular coverage or
a round trip to any server, because the whole point is working in the half
second before an accident, not the several seconds a network round trip
would take.
</Plain>

## Two competing radio technologies

V2X has, for most of its history, been fought over by two incompatible
radio standards, both of which solve the same latency problem differently:

**DSRC** (Dedicated Short-Range Communications) is a Wi-Fi-derived standard
(802.11p) — a direct, ad-hoc radio link between vehicles with no
infrastructure required at all.

**C-V2X** (Cellular V2X) reuses cellular radio technology but in a mode
called **PC5** (sidelink) that lets two devices talk directly to each other
without going through a cell tower — getting DSRC's low latency while
sharing silicon and spectrum expertise with the vehicle's existing cellular
modem.

Momentum has shifted toward C-V2X in most major markets, largely because it
shares a hardware and standards lineage with the 4G/5G modem the vehicle
already carries for the telematics use cases covered earlier in this
module — but a platform engineer's job is the same regardless of which one
a given programme has committed to.

## Where this sits relative to everything else in this module

Every other connectivity topic assumes cellular or Bluetooth transport, both
of which have latencies in the tens to hundreds of milliseconds and neither
of which guarantees delivery. V2X messages — a "hard braking ahead" alert or
a signal-phase-and-timing broadcast from an intersection — need to arrive
reliably within a budget closer to 100 ms end to end, and they need to keep
arriving even where cellular coverage is poor, since PC5/802.11p sidelink
does not depend on it at all.

```text title="Where a V2X safety message actually goes"
Vehicle B: hard braking event detected
  → V2X radio broadcasts a Basic Safety Message over PC5/sidelink
    (no cell tower, no server round trip)
  → Vehicle A's V2X radio receives it within the same radio range
  → message is decoded and handed to a safety-relevant application —
    typically NOT running on the same AP as Android at all
  → if relevant, a warning is surfaced to the driver through Android's
    UI, but the safety-critical decision happened below it
```

## Where Android sits: firmly outside the critical path

This should feel familiar from `adas-integration` and `digital-key` earlier
in this curriculum — it is the same pattern repeated a third time. The
safety-critical decision (is this braking event close enough and fast
enough to warrant an emergency warning) runs on a dedicated, real-time,
often ASIL-rated component with a direct radio link, not on Android. AAOS's
role is almost always downstream: receiving an already-decided alert and
presenting it, logging V2X events for later use, or exposing coarse
non-safety-critical V2X-derived data (like an approaching-hazard flag) as an
ordinary vehicle property through VHAL.

<Analogy title="A separate nervous system for reflexes">
The same "the reflex does not wait for the brain" pattern that showed up
with ADAS and digital key applies again: a V2X collision warning has to fire
faster than anything routed through a general-purpose OS and its UI stack
can guarantee. Android's role is to explain the reflex to the driver after
it has already happened at the layer built specifically to be fast enough.
</Analogy>

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A team is asked to add "V2X alerts" to an infotainment app and starts by
subscribing to a vehicle property for hard-braking events from a vehicle
ahead — reasonable, since that is exactly how every other vehicle signal in
this curriculum has been consumed. The requirement quietly changes shape
once it becomes clear the underlying safety warning already fires through a
dedicated ADAS/V2X safety pathway with its own audible and haptic alert,
independent of anything Android does. The app's actual job is not "detect
the hazard" — it is "log it, and optionally show supplementary detail after
the safety-critical warning has already happened." Scoping the app around
the wrong half of that distinction is the same mistake `adas-integration`
already warned about, showing up again in a new feature.
</Scenario>

<Recap>
- V2X is direct vehicle-to-vehicle/infrastructure/pedestrian radio
  communication, specifically because a cellular round trip through a
  server is too slow for a pre-collision warning.
- DSRC and C-V2X are the two competing radio standards; C-V2X's PC5 sidelink
  mode has gained ground in most markets by sharing lineage with the
  vehicle's existing cellular modem.
- V2X messages need reliable delivery in roughly a 100 ms budget and must
  keep working without cellular coverage — neither Bluetooth nor cellular,
  as covered elsewhere in this module, can offer both.
- Android sits downstream of the safety-critical decision, the same
  boundary already seen with ADAS and digital key: presenting an alert
  that a dedicated, faster component already decided to raise.
</Recap>
