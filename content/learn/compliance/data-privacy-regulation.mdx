`cybersecurity-regulation` covered UN R155 — a process requirement about
whether the vehicle can be attacked. This topic is about a different, older
body of law that also lands squarely on the platform layer: whether the
vehicle is allowed to collect what it collects, tell the driver about it, and
hand it over on request. The connectivity module's telemetry checklist gave
the practical rules; this is the regulatory reasoning underneath them, and
why the checklist looks the way it does.

<Plain>
Cybersecurity regulation asks "can someone break in." Privacy regulation
asks "should we have that data at all, and does the person it's about know
we have it." A vehicle can be perfectly secure and still be non-compliant —
security and privacy are two separate legal obligations, checked by
different people, and a fix for one does not automatically satisfy the
other.
</Plain>

## What makes a vehicle a privacy problem at all

A modern connected vehicle continuously produces location, driving-behaviour
and usage data — much of which counts as **personal data** under GDPR or
**personal information** under CCPA the moment it can be linked to an
identifiable person, which a VIN, an account, or even a consistent driving
pattern usually can. That reclassifies what looks like ordinary telemetry
into data with legal obligations attached: a lawful basis for collecting it,
a stated purpose it may only be used for, a retention limit, and — under
GDPR specifically — rights the data subject can exercise, including access,
correction, and erasure.

| Regime | Applies when | What it grants the driver |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GDPR (EU/EEA) | Vehicle or fleet operator processes data of an EU resident | Access, correction, erasure, portability, objection to processing |
| CCPA/CPRA (California) | Business meets state thresholds and processes a California resident's data | Right to know, delete, correct, opt out of sale/sharing |
| Sector-specific (varies) | Some markets treat vehicle location/telemetry as a distinct regulated category | Often stricter consent and localisation rules than the general law |

## The distinction that actually drives the design work

GDPR separates a **data controller** (decides why data is processed) from a
**data processor** (processes it on the controller's behalf). An OEM is
almost always the controller; a supplier providing, say, the telematics
platform is often a processor. This is not abstract legal structuring — it
determines who has to answer a subject-access or erasure request, and
therefore who the platform's data-handling code has to be able to actually
serve. A "delete my data" request that legally has thirty days to be
fulfilled is worthless if the vehicle's software has no mechanism to locate
and delete a specific driver's data across every place it has landed —
on-device caches, uploaded telemetry, and any downstream analytics store.

<Analogy title="A tenant's request to a landlord who used a contractor">
If a tenant asks a landlord to remove their information from a system, the
landlord (controller) is legally on the hook to make it happen — even if the
actual data sits in a contractor's (processor's) database. The landlord has
to already know exactly where that contractor put it, or the promise made to
the tenant cannot be kept. A vehicle programme that has not mapped every
place a piece of personal data ends up is in the landlord's position without
having asked the contractor where anything is.
</Analogy>

## Where this touches AAOS specifically

Two things on the platform side matter more than anything else here.
**Per-profile isolation** — the headless system user and multi-user model
covered in the framework module — is a genuine privacy asset, not just a
UX feature: it is what makes it technically possible to delete one driver's
data without touching a co-driver's. Building a feature on the *shared*
system user because it is simpler quietly removes that option later.
**On-device aggregation before upload** — summarising rather than
transmitting raw location traces — is the single most effective thing a
platform can do to reduce what counts as personal data in the first place,
because data that never leaves the vehicle in identifiable form was never
exposed to most of the compliance burden that applies once it does.

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A fleet-analytics feature ships correctly from a security standpoint —
encrypted in transit, access-controlled at rest — and still fails a privacy
review, because it uploads raw per-second GPS traces tagged by VIN with no
retention limit and no mechanism to delete one vehicle's history on request.
Nothing about the implementation was insecure. The problem is that the
feature was designed to answer "is this data protected," when the question
that needed answering first was "should this data exist in this form at
all, for this long." Aggregating to per-trip summaries and adding a
retention job addresses both the legal exposure and, incidentally, the
storage cost the earlier performance topic in this curriculum would also
have flagged.
</Scenario>

<Callout type="note" title="Consent has to be revocable at runtime, not just given once">
A driver who consented to location sharing when the vehicle was purchased
has to be able to withdraw it later, and the withdrawal has to actually stop
new collection — a setting that only affects future units sold, not the
running software on vehicles already in the field, does not satisfy either
GDPR or CCPA.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- Privacy regulation and cybersecurity regulation are separate legal
  obligations answering different questions; passing one says nothing about
  the other.
- Telemetry becomes regulated personal data the moment it can be linked to
  an identifiable person — which a VIN or a consistent driving pattern
  usually can, even without a name attached.
- The controller/processor split determines who is legally on the hook for
  a deletion or access request, and therefore what the platform's
  data-handling code actually has to be capable of doing on demand.
- Per-profile isolation and on-device aggregation before upload are the two
  platform-level decisions that most directly reduce privacy exposure —
  building around the shared system user, or uploading raw rather than
  aggregated data, quietly removes that option later.
- Consent must be revocable at runtime and actually stop collection
  immediately — a setting that only applies to future builds does not meet
  the bar.
</Recap>
