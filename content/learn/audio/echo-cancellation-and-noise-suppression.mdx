A hands-free call sounds fine to the driver and terrible to the person on the
other end, who keeps hearing their own words echoed back a fraction of a
second late over road noise. Routing is correct — the mic is picking up the
right signal, on the right bus, at the right gain. The problem this topic
covers sits one layer below all of that: what happens to the signal between
the microphone and the network, before routing ever gets involved.

<Plain>
In a cabin, the call's own output is playing on the same speakers the
microphone can hear. Without correction, the far end hears themselves
delayed by however long it took the sound to leave the speaker, bounce
around the cabin, and re-enter the mic — a classic echo. **Acoustic echo
cancellation (AEC)** models that acoustic path and subtracts the predicted
echo from the mic signal before it is ever sent. **Noise suppression (NS)**
is a separate, unrelated step that reduces road, wind and HVAC noise picked
up by the same microphone.
</Plain>

## Where this sits in the signal path

AEC and NS are **pre-processing effects** applied to the *capture* path —
between the microphone HAL and whatever consumes the signal (the modem stack
for a cellular call, the voice-assistant pipeline for wake-word detection) —
not the playback path that routing and ducking govern. They run as an
`AudioEffect` chain attached to an `AudioRecord` session, most commonly
implemented in native code in the platform's pre-processing effects bundle
and exposed to apps through the public `AcousticEchoCanceler` and
`NoiseSuppressor` effect classes.

```text title="Capture-side processing, roughly"
speaker output  ──────────────┐
                               │ (acoustic path: bounces around the cabin)
microphone  ── raw capture ──►│
                               ▼
                    AEC: subtract predicted echo
                         (needs a copy of what the speaker is playing)
                               │
                               ▼
                    NS: suppress road/wind/HVAC noise
                               │
                               ▼
                    processed signal → modem stack / voice pipeline
```

The detail worth sitting with: **AEC needs to know what the speaker is
playing**, at the same time it is processing what the mic heard, in order to
subtract it. That reference signal has to be routed to the effect
correctly and with the right timing — which is exactly the kind of thing
that is invisible until it is wrong.

## Why AEC is the one that breaks quietly

Noise suppression failing produces an obviously noisy call — easy to notice,
easy to report. AEC failing produces something much harder to self-diagnose:
the *driver* hears nothing wrong, because they are not the one hearing the
echo. Only the far-end party experiences it, and by the time that feedback
reaches an engineer it has passed through a support ticket, a vague
description, and often weeks of delay.

<Analogy title="A one-way mirror on the wrong wall">
Echo cancellation is like a soundproofed booth: from inside, everything
sounds normal, because the booth is doing its job of stopping the sound from
looping back. If the booth's seal is broken, the person inside genuinely
cannot tell — they hear their own voice exactly once, same as always. It is
only the person listening from outside who hears it twice.
</Analogy>

## Common bring-up failures

| Symptom | Usual cause |
| --- | --- |
| Far end hears a full, undamped echo | AEC reference signal not routed to the effect at all |
| Echo present only above a certain volume | Reference path clipping or gain mismatch between actual playback level and what AEC is told the level is |
| Echo returns after a media-to-call handoff | Reference signal still pointing at the previous audio source after routing changed |
| Voice sounds robotic or clipped | NS tuned too aggressively for cabin noise floor, suppressing speech along with noise |
| Wake-word detection works in a quiet cabin, fails at speed | NS or AEC not applied to the assistant's capture session at all — a different `AudioRecord` than the call's |

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A call-quality regression appears only after a media-playback-to-call
handoff, never on a call placed from an idle cabin. The call's AEC is
correctly configured; the reference signal it receives is simply the wrong
one — still wired to the media stream that was routing to the speakers a
moment earlier, because the handoff swapped the *audible* output but nobody
updated which stream the effect chain treats as its echo reference. The two
routing decisions — what plays, and what AEC is told is playing — are
configured in different places, and it is entirely possible to get one right
and the other stale.
</Scenario>

<Callout type="tip" title="Test with a real acoustic loop, not a synthetic signal">
AEC tuning that looks perfect against a directly-injected test tone can still
fail in the vehicle, because the actual acoustic path — cabin geometry,
speaker placement, seat foam absorption — is what the algorithm has to model.
Validate with a real microphone hearing a real speaker in the actual cabin,
not a wire-level loopback.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- AEC and NS are pre-processing effects on the **capture** path, separate
  from the routing and ducking decisions covered earlier in this module.
- AEC needs an accurate, correctly-timed copy of what is currently playing
  to subtract it from the mic signal — get that reference signal wrong and
  cancellation silently fails.
- The driver cannot self-diagnose a broken AEC — only the far-end party
  hears the echo — which is why this class of bug tends to arrive as a
  vague, delayed complaint rather than an obvious local symptom.
- A stream-routing change (like a media-to-call handoff) can leave the AEC
  reference pointing at the wrong source even when the audible routing is
  correct.
- Tune and validate against the real acoustic path in the vehicle; a
  synthetic test-tone loopback will not reveal cabin-specific echo.
</Recap>
