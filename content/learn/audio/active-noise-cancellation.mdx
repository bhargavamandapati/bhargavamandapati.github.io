`echo-cancellation-and-noise-suppression` covered a capture-side problem:
removing a copy of the cabin's own playback from what the microphone picks
up. Active Noise Cancellation solves a completely different problem, on the
playback side — cancelling a noise source the cabin's audio system never
produced at all, and never has a clean reference copy of, because it comes
from the engine and the road rather than from a speaker.

<Plain>
ANC generates an **anti-noise** waveform — the inverse of the noise it is
trying to cancel — and plays it through the cabin's speakers so it destructively
interferes with the real noise at the point it reaches an ear. It requires
constant, real-time knowledge of what the noise actually sounds like right
now, which is the entire difficulty: unlike echo cancellation's reference
signal (the exact audio the platform just told a speaker to play), road
noise has no clean source signal to subtract — it has to be *measured*,
continuously, and predicted just far enough ahead to cancel it before it
arrives.
</Plain>

## Why this is a harder problem than it sounds

Echo cancellation has an enormous advantage: the reference signal is exact
and known in advance, because the platform itself generated it. ANC has
none of that. Road and engine noise is measured by microphones placed near
where cancellation needs to happen, and by the time that measurement is
processed and an anti-noise waveform is generated and played, the real
noise has moved on — sound travels fast enough that even a few milliseconds
of processing latency can mean the anti-noise waveform arrives out of phase
with the noise it was meant to cancel, at which point it does not reduce
the noise, it adds to it.

```text title="The latency budget ANC has to beat"
noise generated at its source (tyre, road surface, engine)
  → travels to the reference microphone
  → DSP predicts what it will sound like at the driver's ear
  → generates the inverse waveform
  → plays it through the cabin speakers
  → must arrive at the ear within a few milliseconds of the real noise,
    or the cancellation is wrong more than it is right
```

## Feedforward and feedback: two different microphone strategies

**Feedforward** ANC places a reference microphone near the noise source
(inside a wheel arch, for engine-adjacent noise near the firewall) and
predicts what that noise will sound like once it reaches the cabin,
generating anti-noise ahead of time. It reacts faster because the
microphone hears the noise before it reaches the driver, but its prediction
model has to account for how the noise changes as it travels from that
microphone's location to the ear.

**Feedback** ANC places a microphone close to where cancellation actually
needs to happen — near the headrest — and continuously adjusts the
anti-noise signal based on what is still audible there. It measures the
result directly rather than predicting it, which is more accurate but
reacts to noise that has, by definition, already partially arrived.

<Analogy title="Predicting rain versus watching the puddle form">
Feedforward is checking the weather radar upwind and opening an umbrella
before the rain reaches you — fast, but only as good as the prediction.
Feedback is watching a puddle already forming at your feet and reacting to
it directly — accurate, but by definition after the fact. Most real ANC
systems combine both, the way a good umbrella habit combines checking the
forecast with noticing the first drops.
</Analogy>

## Why it works well on engine drone and poorly on road roar

ANC is highly effective against **low-frequency, periodic** noise — engine
drone at a fairly stable RPM is close to a predictable waveform, which is
exactly the case the anti-noise math handles well. It is far less effective
against **broadband, chaotic** noise — road roar over coarse asphalt is
closer to random noise across a wide frequency range, with no stable
waveform to predict or cancel cleanly. This is why cabin ANC marketing
tends to demonstrate engine noise or a specific droning frequency rather
than a poor road surface at speed: the physics genuinely favours one case
over the other, not a limitation of any particular implementation.

| Noise type | ANC effectiveness | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engine drone, steady RPM | High | Low-frequency, near-periodic, predictable |
| Road roar, coarse surface | Low to moderate | Broadband, chaotic, poorly predictable |
| Wind noise at speed | Low | Broadband and highly variable with speed and window seals |
| A single resonant frequency (e.g. a drivetrain harmonic) | High | Narrowband and stable — the easiest case for ANC |

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A cabin ANC feature is validated on a smooth test track and ships with
strong measured noise reduction. Customer feedback after launch describes
the feature as "barely noticeable" on the actual roads most owners drive.
The test track's surface noise is close to periodic at a steady speed —
exactly the case ANC handles best — while everyday roads are the broadband,
chaotic case it handles worst. The fix is not a DSP bug; it is validating
against representative road surfaces from the start, and setting the
feature's own marketing and UX copy to reflect what the physics can
actually promise, rather than the test track's best case.
</Scenario>

## Where this sits relative to everything else in this module

ANC is a **playback-side, speaker-driven** feature — it uses the same
output buses `car-audio-architecture` already covers, and its anti-noise
signal has to be mixed and routed like any other audio context, correctly
ducked or not ducked depending on what else is playing. It is entirely
independent of the capture-side AEC/NS pipeline; a vehicle can run both
simultaneously, cancelling road noise on the way out and echo on the way
in, without either one being aware the other exists.

Unlike AEC/NS, though, ANC has no public AOSP framework API of its own —
there is no `ANC` entry in CarAudioService's context list the way there is
for `MUSIC` or `NAVIGATION`. Cabin ANC is almost always a dedicated
amplifier or DSP feature sitting outside the Android audio HAL's context
model entirely, not a stream Android routes and ducks like any other.

<Callout type="tip" title="ANC and a driver-facing microphone can interact in a way worth testing explicitly">
An ANC reference microphone placed near the driver can pick up the driver's
own voice during a hands-free call, and the call's own AEC pipeline was not
designed with an anti-noise signal from a *different* system also playing
through the same speakers. Test hands-free call quality specifically with
ANC active, not only with it disabled, since the two systems were very
likely developed and validated independently.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- ANC cancels noise the audio system never produced — engine and road noise
  — by generating a real-time inverse waveform, unlike echo cancellation's
  exact, pre-known reference signal.
- Latency is the entire difficulty: an anti-noise waveform that arrives
  even a few milliseconds late adds to the noise instead of cancelling it.
- Feedforward microphones near the noise source react faster but predict;
  feedback microphones near the ear react slower but measure directly —
  most real systems combine both.
- ANC is strong against steady, low-frequency noise like engine drone and
  weak against broadband, chaotic noise like road roar — a physics
  limitation, not an implementation gap, and worth validating against real
  road surfaces rather than a smooth test track.
</Recap>
