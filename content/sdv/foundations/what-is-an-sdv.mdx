Almost every car company now says they are building software-defined vehicles.
The phrase appears in press releases, job adverts and architecture documents,
and it is used so loosely that it has nearly stopped meaning anything.

So let us pin it down.

## The definition that is actually useful

<Plain>
A vehicle is **software-defined** when what the car *does* is determined mostly by
software running on general-purpose computers, rather than by fixed behaviour
built into dedicated hardware.

The practical test: **can the vehicle gain a new capability after it has been
sold, without changing any hardware?**

If yes, in a meaningful way — not just a new wallpaper — it is software-defined.
If no, it is a conventional vehicle that happens to contain software.
</Plain>

Note what is *not* in that definition. Nothing about touchscreens, connectivity,
electric drivetrains or artificial intelligence. Those often accompany SDV, and
none of them is what the term means.

## How cars were built before

To see what changed, look at how it worked for thirty years.

A car maker decides it wants heated seats. An engineer writes a specification. A
supplier builds a small computer — an <T>ECU</T> — that does exactly that: reads
a switch, reads a temperature sensor, drives a heating element.

That ECU is delivered, tested, and bolted into the car. Its software is burned in
and essentially never changes. Multiply by a hundred functions and you get a
vehicle with a hundred small computers, each doing one thing, wired together.

<Analogy title="Like a kitchen full of single-purpose appliances">
A toaster toasts. A kettle boils. A blender blends. Each does its one job
reliably and none of them can learn a new one. If you want to make soup, you buy
another appliance.

That is a conventional vehicle. Each function has its own box, and adding a
function means adding a box.
</Analogy>

<Plain>
This was not a bad design. It was an excellent one for its constraints:
components were cheap, functions were independent, and a failure in one thing did
not affect another.

It stopped being the right answer when the number of functions exploded and
customers started expecting the car to improve after they bought it.
</Plain>

## What replaces it

<Analogy title="Like replacing the appliances with a computer and a robot arm">
Now imagine one capable machine that can be *told* how to toast, boil or blend.
Making soup becomes a software update, not a purchase.

That is the software-defined vehicle. Fewer, more powerful computers, and
functions expressed as software running on them.
</Analogy>

## The four things that actually change

Strip away the marketing and SDV means four specific engineering shifts.

### 1. Fewer, more powerful computers

A hundred small controllers becomes a handful of powerful ones, with simple
**zone controllers** near the wiring doing little more than connecting things up.

This is the change that makes everything else possible, and it is a hardware
change — which is why SDV cannot be retrofitted to an existing platform.

### 2. Functions become software, not boxes

Heated seats stop being "the seat ECU" and become a piece of software running on
a shared computer, alongside forty other pieces of software.

<Plain>
This is a bigger change than it sounds. Once a function is software on a shared
machine, it can be updated, replaced, moved to a different processor, or removed
— none of which is true of a box bolted behind the dashboard.
</Plain>

### 3. Components offer services instead of broadcasting signals

The old model: an ECU shouts values onto a shared wire and anything interested
listens. Nobody is addressed and nothing replies.

The new model: a component **offers a service** that others discover at runtime
and call, with requests, responses and errors.

<Plain>
Why it matters: in the old model, adding a new consumer of a signal means
re-doing the network design. In the new model, adding a consumer means writing
software that calls an existing service — and nothing else changes.

That single difference is most of what allows features to be added after the car
ships.
</Plain>

### 4. Software is delivered continuously

Software stops being finished at start of production. Updates arrive over the
air, throughout the vehicle's life, which means the development process has to
keep running for fifteen years rather than stopping at launch.

<Scenario title="The same feature, both ways">
**Before.** A customer wants automatic high beams. Retrofitting means a new
camera, a new ECU, a wiring change and a workshop visit. In practice it is not
offered.

**After.** The camera is already there for other functions. The compute is
already there. The feature is software that reads the existing camera service and
calls the existing lighting service. It arrives as an update while the car sits
on the driveway.

The customer experience is completely different, and the enabling change was
architectural, not clever.
</Scenario>

## What SDV is not

<Plain>
Being blunt about this saves a lot of confused conversations.

**Not the same as electric.** Combustion cars can be software-defined; plenty of
EVs are not. The two happen to be arriving together because both need new
platforms.

**Not the same as connected.** A car with a data connection and a hundred fixed
ECUs is connected, not software-defined.

**Not the same as a big touchscreen.** The screen is the most visible part and
the least relevant to the definition.

**Not the same as autonomous.** Self-driving needs SDV foundations; SDV does not
imply any autonomy at all.
</Plain>

## How to tell whether a claim is real

Four questions that cut through it quickly:

1. **How many general-purpose computers does the vehicle have?** If it is still
   dozens of fixed-function ECUs, the architecture has not changed.
2. **Can a new feature be added without new hardware?** Ask for an example that
   actually shipped.
3. **Do components offer services, or broadcast signals?** Ask what happens when
   a new consumer needs an existing value.
4. **Is software still being developed for vehicles already sold?** Or did the
   team disband at start of production?

<Plain>
Answers of "a few", "yes, here is one", "services", and "yes" describe a real SDV
programme. Anything else is a conventional vehicle with a modern user interface —
which is a perfectly reasonable product, just not this one.
</Plain>

## Why anyone is doing this

The engineering is harder, the platforms cost more, and the organisational change
is painful. Three reasons it is happening anyway:

**Complexity became unmanageable.** A hundred ECUs from thirty suppliers, each
with its own release cycle, is not integrable at the rate customers now expect
features.

**Customers expect improvement.** A phone gets better after purchase. A car that
does not now feels dated within two years.

**Revenue after the sale.** Features, subscriptions and services sold to a vehicle
already on the road — a genuinely new business model, and the one most often
cited internally.

The next topic goes into these forces properly, because they explain most of the
decisions you will otherwise find arbitrary.

<Recap>
- A vehicle is **software-defined** when its capabilities come from software on
  general-purpose computers, and can meaningfully change after it is sold.
- Four concrete shifts: **fewer, bigger computers**; **functions as software**;
  **services instead of broadcast signals**; **continuous delivery**.
- SDV is **not** the same as electric, connected, touchscreen-equipped or
  autonomous.
- The sharpest test: **can a new capability arrive without new hardware?**
- The move is driven by **unmanageable complexity, customer expectation, and
  revenue after the sale** — not by technology enthusiasm.
</Recap>
