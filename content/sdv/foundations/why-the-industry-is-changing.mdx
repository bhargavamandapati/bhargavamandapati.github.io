Rewriting a vehicle's entire electrical architecture is an enormous, risky,
expensive undertaking. Car makers are conservative organisations. So why is
essentially the whole industry doing it at once?

Four pressures. Once you know them, most of the technical decisions you will
meet stop looking arbitrary.

## Pressure 1 — Complexity stopped being manageable

<Scenario title="The integration problem that has no solution">
A vehicle programme has 120 <T>ECU</T>s from 40 suppliers. Each supplier has its
own release cycle, tooling and test process.

A single feature — say, "the seat moves back when you unlock the car" —
touches the key module, the body controller, the seat ECU and the gateway. Four
suppliers, four release schedules.

Changing it means coordinating four organisations, four validation cycles and
four delivery dates. This takes months, and it takes months **every single
time**.

There is no clever process that fixes this. The cost is structural: it comes from
the number of independent parties, and the only way to reduce it is to reduce
that number.
</Scenario>

<Plain>
This is the pressure engineers feel most directly. Consolidating a hundred
independently-delivered boxes into a handful of computers running software you
control turns a four-organisation negotiation into a code change.

It is the least glamorous reason for SDV and probably the most important one.
</Plain>

## Pressure 2 — Customers now expect improvement

A phone gets better after you buy it. So does a television, a games console, a
watch.

A car, historically, did not. It was as good on day one as it would ever be, and
slightly worse every year after.

<Plain>
Once one manufacturer demonstrated that a car could gain features overnight, that
became the baseline expectation rather than a differentiator. A vehicle that
cannot improve now reads as dated within two years of purchase — which affects
resale value, which affects lease pricing, which affects what customers will pay.

The competitive pressure here is not about any specific feature. It is about
whether the product feels alive.
</Plain>

## Pressure 3 — Revenue after the sale

Traditionally a car generates revenue once, at the point of sale, plus servicing.

Software changes that. A feature can be sold to a vehicle already on the road.
Subscriptions, one-off unlocks, upgrades — all delivered without anybody visiting
a workshop.

<Plain>
This is the reason most often cited in boardrooms, and it is worth being honest
about how it has actually gone.

Some of it has worked well: navigation subscriptions, connected services,
genuinely new capabilities delivered later.

Some of it has gone badly. Charging for heated seats in a car that physically
contains the heating elements produced a significant customer backlash, and
several manufacturers reversed course.

The distinction customers draw is clear even when companies miss it: **paying for
something new is acceptable; paying to unlock something you already own is not.**
<T>Feature on demand</T> covers this properly later in the curriculum.
</Plain>

## Pressure 4 — Regulation now requires update capability

This one is less discussed and increasingly decisive.

<T>Cybersecurity</T> regulation in several major markets now requires
manufacturers to monitor for vulnerabilities and respond throughout a vehicle's
service life — which can be fifteen years.

<Plain>
Consider what that obligation implies. A vulnerability is found in a library used
by a component in a vehicle sold six years ago. You are legally required to
respond.

If the only way to update that component is a workshop visit for every affected
vehicle, the obligation is practically impossible to meet.

**The ability to update over the air stops being a feature and becomes a
compliance requirement.** And once you have built the infrastructure to deliver
security fixes over the air, delivering features over the air is nearly free —
which pulls the rest of SDV along behind it.
</Plain>

## What actually enabled it

Pressures explain the motive. Three things made it possible now rather than a
decade ago:

**Automotive-grade processors got powerful enough.** Running several operating
systems and dozens of functions on one chip, within an automotive thermal and
temperature envelope, is recent.

**Automotive Ethernet matured.** Moving from broadcast <T>CAN</T> at kilobits to
switched Ethernet at gigabits — with <T>TSN</T> providing timing guarantees — is
what allows a central computer to talk to everything fast enough.

**Hypervisors became automotive-viable.** Running a safety-rated system and a
general-purpose one on the same chip, with provable isolation, is what makes
consolidation acceptable to a safety engineer.

## The costs, stated honestly

SDV is not free, and pretending otherwise leads to bad planning.

| Cost | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| Hardware | Central compute is far more expensive than a small ECU |
| Complexity moves | It does not vanish — it becomes software complexity |
| New skills | Hypervisors, service architectures, cloud, CI at scale |
| Organisational change | Suppliers deliver software, not boxes — a different contract |
| Safety burden | Proving isolation on shared hardware is genuinely hard |
| Lifetime obligation | Software teams must exist for fifteen years, not until launch |

<Plain>
That last row is the one most often underestimated. A conventional programme's
software team disbands after start of production. An SDV programme's team must
keep going for the life of the vehicle — a permanent operating cost, not a
project cost.

Organisations built around project funding find this genuinely difficult.
</Plain>

## What this means for your work

Understanding the pressures makes a lot of things predictable:

- **Why architecture reviews are strict about service interfaces.** Because the
  whole point is adding consumers later without redesign.
- **Why the update path is treated as safety-critical.** Because it is a
  compliance obligation, not a convenience.
- **Why dependencies get scrutinised.** Because someone will be patching them in
  2041.
- **Why simulation gets so much investment.** Because physical vehicles are the
  bottleneck and always will be.

<Recap>
- **Complexity** made a hundred independently-delivered ECUs unintegrable at the
  rate features are now expected.
- **Customer expectation** shifted: a car that cannot improve feels dated.
- **Revenue after the sale** is the commercial driver — and the part that has
  caused real backlash when done badly.
- **Regulation** now requires update capability for a vehicle's whole life, which
  makes OTA a compliance matter rather than a feature.
- Enabled by **powerful automotive processors, Automotive Ethernet, and viable
  hypervisors**.
- The costs are real: hardware, skills, organisational change, and a software
  team that must exist for fifteen years.
</Recap>
