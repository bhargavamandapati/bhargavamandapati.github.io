Each platform topic in this module makes its own case from its own vantage
point — AAOS explains where Android fits, AUTOSAR explains Classic versus
Adaptive, QNX explains the safety-island pattern, Linux/AGL explains the
build-your-own-distribution option. None of them is wrong; none of them, on
its own, tells you which one to put in a given zone. That question only has
an answer once all four are on the table together, against one vehicle's
actual zone map.

<Plain>
A modern vehicle is not "an OS" — a zonal or domain architecture typically
runs three to five *different* operating systems simultaneously, one per
zone or domain, each chosen for what that specific zone needs to be good
at. Asking "which platform should we use" without first asking "for which
zone" is the most common way this decision goes wrong.
</Plain>

## The four candidates, and the one question each answers best

| Platform | Answers best | Typical zone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AUTOSAR Classic | Hard real-time, statically configured, ASIL-D capable | Powertrain, braking, steering |
| AUTOSAR Adaptive / QNX | Safety-relevant but dynamically updatable, POSIX-like | ADAS compute, safety-island cluster rendering |
| Android Automotive | Rich UI, app ecosystem, frequent feature updates | Cockpit, infotainment, non-critical cluster layers |
| Linux (AGL or a custom Yocto build) | Full control, no licensing constraints, connectivity-heavy workloads | Gateway, telematics control unit, central compute orchestration |

No zone is forced to pick exactly one of these in isolation — the cluster
topic already covers a genuinely common pattern where a **safety-island**
running QNX or AUTOSAR renders the ASIL-relevant telltales while Android
renders everything else on the same physical panel, composited together.
The decision framework below is for the boundary itself: which parts of a
zone's workload belong on which side of that split.

## Four questions, asked per zone, not per vehicle

**Does anything in this zone need a certified safety case?** If yes, that
part is AUTOSAR Classic or a certified RTOS (QNX, or AUTOSAR Adaptive on a
certified base), full stop — Android's own architecture explicitly
disclaims this territory, and the AAOS topic on where Android is a poor fit
covers exactly why forcing it there does not work.

**How often does this zone's software need to change after the vehicle
ships?** A powertrain control loop is validated once and rarely touched
again; an infotainment recommendation engine changes monthly. The former
tolerates AUTOSAR Classic's static configuration; the latter needs a
platform built around frequent, safe updates, which is precisely Android
Automotive's and AUTOSAR Adaptive's shared design goal, from opposite
starting points.

**Does this zone need a broad third-party app ecosystem, or a small,
audited set of first-party services?** An app ecosystem is Android's
entire reason for existing in a vehicle at all. A zone with no third-party
app story — a gateway routing CAN and Ethernet traffic, say — gains nothing
from Android's weight and gains real value from Linux's minimalism or
AUTOSAR Adaptive's service-oriented model.

**What does the team actually have deep expertise in already?** The
"correct" architectural answer that a team cannot operate safely is worse
than a slightly less optimal one they can. This is the least technical
question and the one most often skipped in favour of the first three — and
skipping it is how a programme ends up running a platform nobody on the
team has shipped before, on a schedule that assumed otherwise.

<Analogy title="Choosing a vehicle for a delivery route, not a vehicle for the fleet">
Choosing "our platform" for the whole vehicle is like choosing one vehicle
type for a company's entire delivery fleet regardless of route — a van for
motorway freight and a van for last-mile city drops are not the same
correct choice, even though both are technically "a van you could use."
Choosing per zone is choosing per route: the powertrain's route needs a
certified, predictable vehicle; the cockpit's route needs one built for
frequent stops and a lot of cargo variety.
</Analogy>

## Where this actually gets decided badly

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
A programme standardises on "one RTOS everywhere" early, before the
infotainment requirements are even fully scoped, reasoning that a single
platform simplifies tooling and hiring. Eighteen months later the
infotainment team is reimplementing a media-session framework, a
notification system, and a third-party app sandbox that Android Automotive
already provides for free — because the platform decision was made at the
vehicle level, once, before anyone had asked the four questions above about
the cockpit zone specifically. The fix is not switching platforms
mid-programme; it is treating "one platform for tooling simplicity" and
"one platform per zone for technical fit" as a trade-off made consciously,
not a default arrived at by skipping the analysis.
</Scenario>

## The boundary is where the real engineering work is

Whichever split a program lands on, the boundary between zones running
different platforms is where most of the actual integration effort goes —
the topics on service-oriented architecture, SOME/IP, and the cluster's
own safety-island pattern all cover pieces of exactly this boundary. Picking
the platforms is a one-time decision, made early. Designing the interface
between them is ongoing work for the life of the programme, and it is worth
weighting the platform decision partly by how clean that boundary will be,
not only by which platform is individually the best fit for each zone in
isolation.

<Callout type="note" title="This decision is expensive to reverse — treat it that way">
Moving a workload from one platform to another after a programme is well
underway costs far more than the original decision did — every driver,
every integration, every certified artifact tied to the old platform has to
be redone. Spend real time on this framework before committing, because
the cost of getting it wrong is not symmetric with the cost of taking an
extra week to decide.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- A vehicle runs several platforms at once, one per zone — the question is
  never "which platform," it is "which platform, for this specific zone."
- Certification need, update frequency, app-ecosystem requirements, and
  actual team expertise are the four questions that decide it — in that
  order of how hard they are to change later.
- The boundary between platforms, not the platforms themselves, is where
  most of the ongoing engineering effort lives — weight the decision partly
  by how clean that boundary will be.
- This decision is expensive to reverse; treat the time spent deciding as
  cheap by comparison.
</Recap>
