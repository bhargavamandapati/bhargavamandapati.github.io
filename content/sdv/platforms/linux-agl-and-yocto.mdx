Android is not the only answer to "what runs the screen". A large part of the
industry runs plain Linux, and understanding why makes the trade-offs of your own
platform much clearer.

## Why not just use a desktop distribution

<Plain>
You cannot ship Ubuntu in a car, and the reasons are worth listing because they
explain the entire tooling ecosystem that follows.

**You must account for every binary.** Regulation and security review require
knowing exactly what is on the device and where it came from. A general-purpose
distribution pulls in thousands of packages you did not choose.

**Licences are a legal exposure.** A GPL-3 component in a locked-down device
creates obligations most OEMs will not accept. You need to control what enters
the image, not discover it later.

**The hardware is fixed and odd.** A specific SoC, a specific display
controller, vendor kernel patches that are not upstream. A distribution built for
generic PCs does not boot on it.

**Storage and boot time are constrained.** A few gigabytes of flash and a
requirement to show something within seconds. Desktop distributions optimise for
neither.

**The lifetime is fifteen years.** You need to rebuild bit-identically in a
decade, long after any distribution's support window.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="Ordering a suit versus buying one">
An off-the-peg suit is quicker and fine for most occasions. But if you need it to
fit an unusual frame, last fifteen years, and come with documentation of every
thread's origin, it gets made to measure.

<T>Yocto</T> is the tailor. It is more work up front, and the result fits exactly
one body.
</Analogy>

## Yocto: building a distribution rather than installing one

Yocto is not a Linux distribution. It is a build system that produces one from
recipes.

<Plain>
A **recipe** describes one piece of software: where the source comes from, what
it depends on, how to configure and compile it, what files end up in the image.

A **layer** is a collection of recipes — a BSP layer for your SoC, a layer for
your OEM's software, a layer for a middleware stack.

An **image** is a named set of packages to include.

You run `bitbake`, it works out the dependency graph, and hours later you have a
flashable image containing exactly what you specified and nothing else.
</Plain>

```
meta                (Yocto core recipes)
meta-poky           (reference distro policy)
meta-openembedded   (community recipes)
meta-<soc-vendor>   (kernel, bootloader, GPU drivers for your chip)
meta-agl            (automotive services and profiles)
meta-<oem>          (your applications, your branding, your policy)
                    ↓
                 bitbake
                    ↓
       image + SDK + licence manifest + SBOM
```

<Gotcha>
The output that surprises people is not the image — it is the **licence manifest**
and <T>SBOM</T>. Yocto emits a full account of every component and its licence,
because that account is a shipping requirement. Getting it for free is one of the
strongest practical arguments for the whole approach.
</Gotcha>

### The cost, stated plainly

A full Yocto build from cold is measured in hours and tens of gigabytes. Adding
one library can trigger a large rebuild. New engineers find it opaque for weeks.

<Scenario title="A day with Yocto">
You need to add a small JSON library to the image.

1. Write a recipe: source URL, checksum, licence, build steps.
2. Add it to an image or a package group.
3. `bitbake your-image` — and wait, because your change invalidated a dependency
   the sysroot was built on.
4. Flash and test.

Total: most of a day the first time, twenty minutes once you know the layout.

Compare with Android, where adding a dependency to an app is a line in a Gradle
file. The Android experience is better for application work. The Yocto experience
gives you something Android does not: you can also change the kernel, the
bootloader, and the init sequence in the same build with the same tooling.
</Scenario>

## AGL: the shared baseline

Every OEM building automotive Linux was solving the same problems separately.
<T>AGL</T> is the Linux Foundation project that pooled that work.

<Plain>
AGL provides a Yocto-based reference distribution plus a set of automotive
services — audio routing, vehicle signal access, application framework, an
identity model, a Wayland-based compositor with automotive window management.

The pitch is that roughly 70% of a cockpit stack is undifferentiated plumbing
that no customer will ever notice. Sharing it means each OEM's effort goes into
the 30% that is actually their product.
</Plain>

Things AGL contributes that you would otherwise write:

| Piece | What it handles |
|---|---|
| **agl-compositor** | Wayland compositor with automotive policy — which surface may be on top, and when |
| **Application framework** | Launching, sandboxing and permissioning applications |
| **Audio (PipeWire/WirePlumber)** | Routing, priority and ducking between sources |
| **KUKSA integration** | Vehicle signals as <T>VSS</T>, through a <T>data broker</T> |
| **Demo platform** | A bootable reference on common dev boards |

<Plain>
Notice how closely those map onto Android concepts: the compositor is
SurfaceFlinger's job, the application framework is ActivityManager's, the audio
policy is <T>audio focus</T>, and the KUKSA integration plays the role of the
VHAL and <T>Car Service</T>.

They are the same problems. Every cockpit platform has to solve them, and there
are only so many sensible ways to do so.
</Plain>

## Android versus Linux, decided honestly

| | **Android Automotive** | **Automotive Linux / AGL** |
|---|---|---|
| App ecosystem | Play or APK sideload, huge developer pool | Build your own, or web apps |
| Time to a working UI | Fast — the framework is there | Slower — more assembly |
| Control over the stack | Bounded by AOSP's shape | Total |
| Licence exposure | Apache-2 base, Google terms for GAS | You choose every component |
| Memory and storage floor | High | Low, and tunable |
| Boot time | Harder to shorten | Easier to shorten |
| Graphics maturity | Excellent, phone-grade | Good, more integration work |
| Multi-user, multi-display | Built in | You design it |
| Talent pool | Very large | Smaller, more specialised |
| Google relationship | Required for GAS | None |
| Safety certification | Not for the framework | Not for stock Linux either |

<Plain>
Read the last two rows together and you have most of the real decision. An OEM
that wants Play, Maps and Assistant accepts a relationship with Google and
Android's shape. An OEM that treats the cockpit as brand-defining and wants no
dependency chooses Linux and pays for it in engineering.

Both are rational. Which is right depends on whether the cockpit is a
differentiator for that brand or a cost centre.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="Two OEMs, two defensible answers">
**OEM A** sells high volumes in markets where buyers expect their phone
ecosystem in the car. Cockpit software is not where the brand competes. They pick
Android Automotive with GAS, ship familiar apps in eighteen months, and put their
engineering into powertrain and ADAS.

**OEM B** sells premium vehicles where the interior *is* the product. They want a
UI nobody else can have, no third-party dependency on the customer relationship,
and control over every byte. They build on Yocto with AGL components, spend three
years and a much larger team, and end up with something distinctive.

Neither made a mistake. They optimised different things.
</Scenario>

## Where both approaches meet

The interesting development is that below the UI, the two converge.

<Plain>
Both consume vehicle data as <T>VSS</T>. Both increasingly go through a
<T>data broker</T>. Both talk <T>SOME/IP</T> or <T>DDS</T> to the rest of the
vehicle. Both run as a guest on a <T>hypervisor</T> next to a safety partition.

That convergence is genuinely useful to you. A vehicle-abstraction design, a
signal model, and a test harness built around a broker transfer between the two
almost unchanged. Only the top layer — Activities and Views versus Wayland
surfaces — is platform-specific.

If your career is in cockpit software, invest in the layer that transfers.
</Plain>

<Recap>
- A desktop distribution cannot ship in a car: licence exposure, unknown
  binaries, odd hardware, tight storage, fifteen-year support.
- **Yocto builds a distribution from recipes**, giving exact control plus a
  licence manifest and SBOM as build output.
- Its cost is real — long builds, steep learning curve — and its payoff is total
  control of kernel, bootloader and image.
- **AGL** shares the undifferentiated 70%: compositor, app framework, audio,
  vehicle signals via KUKSA.
- AGL's components map almost one-to-one onto Android's — the problems are the
  same.
- The **Android/Linux choice** is mostly about ecosystem versus control, and both
  answers are defensible.
- Below the UI the two **converge** on VSS, brokers and service-oriented
  communication, so that knowledge transfers.
</Recap>
