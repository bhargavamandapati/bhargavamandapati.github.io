You already know how <T>AAOS</T> works. What is worth being precise about is
where it sits in a software-defined vehicle, because the boundary is drawn in a
specific place and a lot of confusion comes from misplacing it.

## What Android is actually for here

<Plain>
Android's contribution to an SDV is the **experience layer and its ecosystem**:
rendering, application lifecycle, a permission model users understand, an app
distribution channel, and a very large pool of developers who already know the
APIs.

Its contribution is *not* vehicle control. It does not command actuators, it is
not on the safety path, and it is not the thing that keeps the car running.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="Like the dashboard of a factory">
The factory floor has controllers running machines to the millisecond. The
control room has screens, dashboards, alerts and people making decisions.

Android is the control room. It reads everything, presents it well, and lets
people act — but the actual machine control lives elsewhere, and the factory
keeps running if a screen goes dark.
</Analogy>

## The boundary, drawn precisely

  <SdvStack />

The <T>VHAL</T> is the boundary, and it is a good one because it is narrow and
explicit. Above it, Android sees a typed key-value world of
<T>vehicle properties</T>. Below it, the implementation can be anything.

<Plain>
In a modern SDV the thing below the VHAL is usually **not** a CAN driver. It is a
SOME/IP client, or a DDS subscriber, or a gRPC connection to a service running in
a different virtual machine on the same chip.

That is a meaningful change from earlier vehicles, and it has a practical
consequence: the VHAL's job has shifted from *decoding* to *translating*. Less
bit-twiddling of CAN frames, more mapping between two service vocabularies.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="Following one value across the boundary">
Ambient light level, in a zonal SDV.

1. A photodiode in the front-left zone reports to the zone controller over LIN.
2. The zone controller publishes it as a SOME/IP event — `LightingService`,
   event `AmbientLevel`, in lux.
3. In central compute, the VHAL implementation is a SOME/IP client subscribed to
   that event. On each notification it scales the value and calls
   `onPropertyEvent` for a vendor property.
4. <T>Car Service</T> receives it and fans it out to registered listeners.
5. Your app's `CarPropertyManager` callback fires; the UI switches to night
   theme.

Steps 1–2 are the vehicle's problem. Step 3 is the seam. Steps 4–5 are ordinary
Android.

Notice what the app never learns: that there is a photodiode, that LIN exists,
that SOME/IP exists. It asked for a property and got a number. That opacity is
the design working.
</Scenario>

## What Android genuinely brings

**An application model that already works.** Lifecycle, permissions, process
isolation, resource management, an installer, a settings framework. Building
that from scratch is years of work that adds no differentiation.

**Distribution.** <T>GAS</T> vehicles get Play, which means an app catalogue
without the OEM negotiating with every developer individually. Even AOSP-only
builds inherit APK packaging and signing.

**Developers.** The pool of engineers who can write an Android app is enormous
compared with the pool who can write a QNX Screen application.

**Multi-display and multi-user out of the box.** <T>Occupant zones</T>, per-user
profiles and multiple displays are platform features rather than a bespoke
subsystem. In a vehicle with front and rear screens this is significant.

**Graphics that keep up.** SurfaceFlinger, hardware composer, GPU compositing and
a mature animation stack. Modern cockpit UI expectations are essentially phone
expectations, and Android already meets them.

## What Android deliberately does not do

<Plain>
**Hard real-time.** Android is not, and does not attempt to be, a real-time
operating system. If something must complete within a bounded time or a person
is harmed, Android is the wrong place for it. This is a design decision, not a
gap awaiting a fix.

**High-ASIL safety.** There is no ISO 26262 certification for AOSP as such. A
safety-rated cluster built on Android achieves that rating by putting the
safety-critical part *somewhere else* — a separate microcontroller, a
safety-certified hypervisor partition, or a QNX guest — and letting Android
handle only the rest.

**Actuation.** Android can request. Something below the VHAL decides whether to
comply. Sending a "set steering angle" property does not move a wheel unless a
vehicle-side component with its own logic and its own safety argument agrees.

**Boot in a legally required time.** Reversing camera imagery is required within
about two seconds in several markets. Android cannot boot that fast from cold.
This is solved by an early-boot path — <T>EVS</T> — that runs before and
independently of the Android framework, or by a separate microcontroller
entirely.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
These are not deficiencies to argue away in a design review. They are the reasons
the architecture around Android looks the way it does. An engineer who
understands *why* the safety island exists is far more useful than one who
proposes moving its function into Android.
</Gotcha>

## Android on the cluster

Increasingly the same Android instance drives both the centre screen and the
<T>cluster</T>. This works, and the way it works is worth being precise about,
because the safety question does not disappear — it gets partitioned.

<Plain>
The pattern nearly everyone converges on:

- **Android renders the informational cluster** — map, media, trip data, the
  parts drivers notice but do not depend on.
- **A safety-rated component renders the mandatory <T>telltales</T>** — brake
  warning, airbag, oil pressure. Often a separate microcontroller compositing on
  top, or a certified guest, or a hardware overlay plane.
- If Android stops, the telltales are **still on screen**, because they never
  came from Android.

The regulator's requirement is that a brake warning is visible. It says nothing
about who draws the album art.
</Plain>

## Where Android is a poor fit

Being honest about this matters more than advocacy.

| Situation | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ASIL D display content | QNX or a safety MCU | Certification evidence already exists |
| Sub-millisecond control loops | AUTOSAR Classic | Deterministic scheduling |
| Sub-second cold boot | RTOS or dedicated MCU | Android's init cannot get there |
| Very constrained hardware | Linux or an RTOS | Android's memory floor is high |
| Full control of every binary | <T>Yocto</T> / <T>AGL</T> | No Google-defined components |
| Vehicle backbone gateway | Adaptive or Linux | Android has no business in the middle |

<Plain>
Notice that none of these are infotainment. In the infotainment slot Android's
trade-offs are close to ideal, which is why it has taken so much of that market.

Outside it, the calculus changes quickly.
</Plain>

## How Android benefits from the SDV shift

The change runs in both directions, and this part often gets missed.

**Ethernet replaces bespoke plumbing.** When the vehicle speaks SOME/IP over
Ethernet, the VHAL becomes a network client instead of a bus decoder. That is a
substantially simpler and more testable thing to write.

**A <T>data broker</T> makes development possible without hardware.** Point the
VHAL at a broker instead of the vehicle, inject values from a script, and the
entire Android stack above becomes testable on a desk. This is the single largest
productivity change available to an AAOS team.

**Consolidation gives Android better hardware.** A cockpit SoC shared with the
cluster and ADAS is far more capable than a dedicated head-unit chip of the
previous generation.

**<T>OTA</T> becomes normal.** Android already has <T>A/B partitions</T> and a
proper update mechanism. In a vehicle programme that has committed to shipping
updates, that maturity is an argument in Android's favour rather than an
inconvenience.

<Scenario title="What changes for you in practice">
On a pre-SDV programme, testing a new "battery preconditioning" screen meant
booking an EV, driving it to a specific state of charge, and hoping the bench
build matched.

On an SDV programme with a broker, it means:

```bash
kuksa-client set Vehicle.Powertrain.TractionBattery.StateOfCharge.Current 18
kuksa-client set Vehicle.CurrentLocation.Latitude 52.52
```

and the screen renders on your desk emulator. You go near a real vehicle only to
confirm the layer *below* the broker.

The Android code you write is the same. The loop you write it in is hours
shorter.
</Scenario>

<Recap>
- Android's role in an SDV is the **experience layer** — rendering, apps,
  permissions, distribution — not vehicle control.
- The **VHAL is the boundary**, and in an SDV its implementation is usually a
  SOME/IP or DDS client rather than a CAN decoder.
- Android brings a working app model, a huge developer pool, multi-display and
  multi-user support, and mature graphics.
- It deliberately does **not** do hard real-time, high-ASIL safety, actuation, or
  two-second cold boot — the architecture around it exists because of that.
- On a shared cluster, **safety content is rendered by something else** and
  composited over Android.
- Android is a poor fit for gateways, control loops and heavily constrained
  hardware — and an excellent fit for infotainment.
- The SDV shift **helps** Android work: simpler VHALs, desk-testable stacks,
  better silicon, and OTA as a first-class expectation.
</Recap>
