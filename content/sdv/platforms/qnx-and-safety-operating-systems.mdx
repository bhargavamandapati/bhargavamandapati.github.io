Every cockpit consolidation project eventually hits the same wall: some content on
the screen is legally required to be there, and Android cannot promise it will
be. <T>QNX</T> and its peers exist for exactly that gap.

## The requirement that forces the issue

<Plain>
A handful of <T>telltales</T> are mandatory. If the brake system has a fault, a
red warning must appear and stay visible. Same for airbags, and for a small
regulated set of others.

"Must" here is a legal obligation, not a product goal. A vehicle that fails it
cannot be type-approved.

Now ask: can you promise that an Android process will draw a specific pixel
pattern, within a bounded time, every time, for fifteen years, including when the
system is under memory pressure and an app is misbehaving?

You cannot promise that. Not because Android is badly built, but because nothing
in its design was ever intended to support that promise, and there is no
certification evidence to back it.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="Like a fire alarm">
A building's lighting can be smart, scheduled, app-controlled and occasionally
buggy. Nobody dies.

The fire alarm is on its own circuit, with its own battery, tested on a schedule,
and deliberately not connected to anything clever. It does one thing and it does
it when everything else has failed.

The safety OS is the fire alarm circuit of the cockpit.
</Analogy>

## What a microkernel actually changes

QNX is a microkernel, and the architectural difference has direct safety
consequences.

```
   MONOLITHIC (Linux, Android)          MICROKERNEL (QNX)

   ┌───────────────────────────┐        ┌────────────┐ ┌────────────┐
   │        applications       │        │    apps    │ │  drivers   │
   ├───────────────────────────┤        │ user space │ │ user space │
   │  drivers │ fs │ net │ mm  │        └──────┬─────┘ └──────┬─────┘
   │        KERNEL SPACE       │               │ message      │
   └───────────────────────────┘        ┌──────┴──────────────┴─────┐
                                        │  microkernel: scheduling, │
   a bad driver panics                  │  IPC, memory protection   │
   the whole system                     └───────────────────────────┘

                                        a bad driver is a process
                                        that can be restarted
```

<Plain>
In Linux, a driver bug is a kernel bug, and a kernel bug takes the system down.

In QNX, a driver is an ordinary user-space process talking to the kernel by
message passing. If it faults, it faults alone. It can be restarted while
everything else keeps running.

The kernel itself is small — tens of thousands of lines rather than tens of
millions. That size is not a bragging point; it is what makes formal analysis and
certification affordable. You can argue about the correctness of something you
can read.
</Plain>

## What "certified" actually means

This phrase gets used loosely, so be precise about what is being bought.

<Plain>
An ISO 26262 certification for an OS is not a claim that the software has no
bugs. It is a package of evidence that:

- The development process followed the standard — requirements traced to code,
  code traced to tests.
- Coverage targets were met at the levels the <T>ASIL</T> requires (MC/DC
  coverage at ASIL D, not merely line coverage).
- Known defects are documented with analysed impact, not quietly fixed.
- A **safety manual** states exactly how the OS must be configured to remain
  within its certified envelope, and what the integrator is still responsible
  for.
- An independent assessor reviewed the whole package.

You are buying **an argument you do not have to construct**, and a supplier who
will stand behind it. On a programme where building that argument yourself would
take years, that is the entire value proposition.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
Certification is conditional. The safety manual will say things like "the
following filesystem is not certified" or "this scheduling mode is out of scope".
Step outside the envelope and the certificate does not cover you.

Teams have discovered late that the configuration they shipped was not the
configuration that was certified. Read the safety manual early, and treat its
constraints as requirements rather than suggestions.
</Gotcha>

## The safety island pattern

Here is how QNX and Android actually coexist, and it is the pattern you will meet
in almost every consolidated cockpit.

>

<Plain>
A certified <T>hypervisor</T> partitions the chip. Each guest gets a fixed
allocation of CPU cores, memory ranges and devices.

- The **safety partition** — QNX, or an RTOS, or in some designs a separate
  microcontroller entirely — renders mandatory telltales and handles anything
  ASIL-rated.
- The **Android partition** renders everything else: navigation, media, the
  informational parts of the cluster.
- The display controller composites the two, with the safety layer on a hardware
  overlay plane that Android cannot touch.

The property being enforced is <T>freedom from interference</T>: nothing Android
does — spinning every core, exhausting memory, crashing — may affect the safety
partition's ability to meet its deadline.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="What the driver sees when Android dies">
A driver is following the map when an update leaves the infotainment stack in a
bad state and Android restarts.

- The map area of the cluster goes blank or shows a fallback.
- The speedometer keeps updating.
- The brake and airbag telltales stay lit exactly as before.
- Nothing about vehicle behaviour changes.
- Thirty seconds later Android is back and the map returns.

The driver experienced a glitch, not a hazard. That outcome is not luck — it is
the partitioning working as designed, and it is the entire reason for the extra
architectural complexity.
</Scenario>

## The alternatives to QNX

QNX has the largest share of this slot but it is not the only option.

| Option | Character | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **QNX** | Certified microkernel, commercial, mature tooling | Clusters, hypervisors, ADAS — the default choice |
| **Integrity / PikeOS / VxWorks** | Certified RTOS, separation-kernel heritage | Avionics-influenced programmes, very high assurance |
| **Safety-certified Linux** | Linux plus a certification package | Attractive licence story, ASIL claims are narrower |
| **AUTOSAR Classic on an MCU** | No OS in the desktop sense | Highest ASIL, small deterministic functions |
| **Zephyr / FreeRTOS** | Small open-source RTOS | Zone controllers, sensors; certification varies |

<Plain>
The pattern across all of them: **the higher the ASIL, the smaller and dumber the
software**. That is not a coincidence. Complexity is the enemy of a safety
argument, so the safest components are deliberately the least capable ones.

This is a useful instinct to carry into design reviews. When someone proposes
adding a feature to the safety partition, the correct first question is whether
it can live outside it instead.
</Plain>

## Why an Android engineer should care

You will not write QNX code. You will still hit its consequences constantly.

**Boot ordering.** The safety partition boots first and fast. Android boots
later and slower. Anything of yours that assumes it is up early is wrong.

**Resource limits are hard limits.** Your partition has N cores and M megabytes,
fixed by configuration. There is no borrowing from the safety partition under
load. Performance work has a ceiling you cannot argue with.

**You do not own the whole display.** The safety overlay sits above your surfaces.
A full-screen layout that assumes it can use every pixel of the cluster will be
partly covered, by design.

**Some properties are read-only to you for a reason.** If a value originates in
the safety domain, Android sees it and cannot set it. That is not a missing
feature request.

**Crashes are contained but visible.** Android restarting is survivable, and it
is also noticeable to the driver. "The partitioning protects us" is not a reason
to be relaxed about stability.

<Scenario title="A design review you will sit in">
Someone proposes drawing the speedometer needle in Android because the animation
would be nicer.

The right response is not "no, because rules". It is to ask which parts are
regulated. Typically: the numeric speed and the mandatory telltales are, and the
surrounding styling is not.

The usual landing point is that the safety partition draws a plain, certified
speed readout and the telltales, while Android draws everything around it. The
result looks like one coherent design and satisfies the requirement.

Getting to that answer requires knowing where the line is — which is why this
topic is worth your time even though you will never open a QNX IDE.
</Scenario>

<Recap>
- Mandatory telltales must be shown reliably, and Android carries no evidence that
  it can promise that.
- QNX is a **microkernel**: drivers run in user space, a fault is contained, and
  the small kernel is what makes certification affordable.
- **Certified** means a package of process, coverage and traceability evidence,
  plus a **safety manual** whose constraints you must actually follow.
- The standard pattern is a **safety island** — certified hypervisor, fixed
  partitions, safety content on a hardware overlay above Android.
- The property enforced is **freedom from interference**: Android cannot affect
  the safety partition, whatever it does.
- Alternatives exist, and across all of them **higher ASIL means smaller, simpler
  software**.
- As an Android engineer this shapes your boot order, your resource budget, your
  display area, and which properties you may write.
</Recap>
