Functional safety is the part of automotive that most obviously has no equivalent
in general software, and it is usually taught through the standard's structure
rather than its purpose. Start with the purpose.

## What it is actually about

<Plain>
Functional safety asks one question: **if this system malfunctions, can it hurt
someone, and what have you done about it?**

That is narrower than it first sounds. It is not about the system being good, or
reliable, or meeting requirements. It is about the specific subset of failures
that lead to physical harm.

A navigation app that crashes is a quality problem. A navigation app that crashes
and takes the speedometer with it is a safety problem — and the difference is
entirely about what else was on that screen.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="Two questions about a ladder">
"Is this a good ladder?" asks about height, weight, how pleasant it is to use.

"Can this ladder drop someone?" is a different question with a different method:
what could fail, how likely, how bad, and what stops it.

ISO 26262 is a rigorous way of asking the second question, and it deliberately
does not care about the first.
</Analogy>

## ASIL, without the ceremony

<T>ASIL</T> ratings come from a hazard analysis of three factors, at the level of
a *situation*, not a component.

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| **Severity** | How badly hurt, if this goes wrong? |
| **Exposure** | How often are people in the situation where it matters? |
| **Controllability** | Can a normal driver cope when it happens? |

<Plain>
Combine those and you get QM (no safety requirement) through ASIL A, B, C to
ASIL D (the most stringent).

The crucial and frequently missed point: **ASIL applies to a hazard, not to a
box**. "The cluster is ASIL B" is shorthand at best. What is actually rated is
something like "loss of the brake warning telltale while driving is ASIL B", and
from that flows a requirement on whatever displays it.

This matters practically because it is what allows decomposition. If the hazard
is "the driver is not warned", and two independent paths could warn them, each
path may carry a lower rating than the combined requirement.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="Rating one cluster, item by item">
Take a single display and work through what it shows.

**Speed reading wrong.** Severity: moderate. Exposure: constant. Controllability:
a driver has other cues — engine noise, traffic, road signs. Typically **ASIL A
or B**.

**Brake fault telltale missing.** Severity: high — the driver keeps driving with
failing brakes. Exposure: rare, but the situation is exactly when it matters.
Controllability: low, since the driver has no other way to know. Typically
**ASIL B or C**.

**Navigation showing the wrong street.** Severity: annoyance. Not a safety
hazard at all. **QM**.

**Media artwork failing to load.** **QM**, obviously.

One physical display, four items, ratings from QM to ASIL C. That spread is the
entire reason for the mixed-criticality architecture — you build to the highest
requirement only where it applies, and let the rest be ordinary software.
</Scenario>

## Why consolidation makes this harder

The SDV promise is fewer, more powerful computers. The safety consequence runs
the other way.

<Plain>
**Separate ECUs give you independence for free.** If the cluster and the media
system are different boxes with different power supplies, a fault in one cannot
plausibly affect the other. You do not have to argue it; the wiring diagram is
the argument.

**Consolidation removes that.** Shared SoC, shared memory controller, shared
power rail, shared thermal envelope, shared bus. Now every claim of independence
must be argued explicitly and demonstrated.

This is the central difficulty of modern cockpit safety, and it is why
<T>freedom from interference</T> is a phrase you will hear constantly.
</Plain>

### What must be shown

| Resource | The question to answer |
|---|---|
| **CPU time** | Can a runaway process starve the safety partition? |
| **Memory** | Can one partition read or corrupt another's? |
| **Peripherals** | Can a guest reprogram a shared controller? |
| **Interrupts** | Can one partition delay another's interrupt handling? |
| **Power** | Does a shared rail create a common-cause failure? |
| **Thermal** | Can heavy load in one partition throttle the other? |
| **Time** | Do both partitions have a trustworthy clock? |

<Plain>
A certified <T>hypervisor</T> answers the first four with hardware enforcement —
fixed core allocation, an IOMMU, static memory partitioning, interrupt routing
that is configured rather than negotiated.

Power and thermal are harder because they are physical. Many designs conclude
that a shared SoC cannot support the highest ASIL content at all, and put a
separate safety microcontroller on the board. That is not a failure of
consolidation; it is consolidation stopping at the point where the argument runs
out.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
"The hypervisor is certified, therefore we have freedom from interference" is
incomplete. The certificate covers the hypervisor **configured as described in
its safety manual**.

If you allocated cores dynamically, enabled a feature the manual excludes, or
shared a device it said not to share, the certificate does not extend to your
system. This gap has been discovered late on real programmes.
</Gotcha>

## The pattern that actually gets built

</DiagramFrame>

<Plain>
The recurring shape:

1. **The safety function is made as small as possible.** Telltales and a numeric
   speed, not a full UI. Small means arguable.
2. **It runs somewhere isolated** — a certified guest, or a separate MCU.
3. **It gets its data independently.** The brake telltale's signal does not pass
   through Android. If it did, Android would be in the safety path and would need
   the rating.
4. **It renders to its own layer**, composited by hardware above Android's
   output.
5. **A watchdog checks it** — the display is actually being updated, the content
   is what was intended, at the expected rate.
6. **Android does everything else**, and its failure is a quality event.

The design instinct to take from this: **the goal is to keep things out of the
safety path, not to make more things safe.**
</Plain>

## What updates do to a safety case

Here is where the SDV genuinely creates a new problem rather than inheriting an
old one.

<Plain>
A safety case is an argument that a **specific version** of a system is
acceptably safe. Traditionally the software was frozen at start of production and
the argument held for the vehicle's life.

If software is going to be updated fifty times over fifteen years, the argument
has to be maintained fifty times. That means:

- **Impact analysis per change.** Does this touch anything the safety argument
  depends on? Answering that requires traceability that most codebases do not
  have by default.
- **Regression evidence.** The safety-relevant tests re-run and re-recorded, every
  release.
- **Configuration control across the fleet.** Which vehicle runs which version,
  and is each combination one you have an argument for?
- **Compatibility with the unchanged parts.** A new cockpit version running
  against a three-year-old body controller must still be safe.
- **R156 documentation.** The regulator expects to see this process, not just its
  output.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="A one-line change and its cost">
An engineer changes the cluster's rendering to composite one extra layer, for a
new media animation.

**The question the process asks:** does this affect the timing of the safety
overlay?

**Finding out:** the overlay is a hardware plane, so in principle no. But the
change increases GPU load, and if GPU load can cause the display controller to
miss a frame deadline, the telltale could be late.

**The work:** measure worst-case frame timing under maximum load, confirm the
margin, record the evidence, update the impact analysis.

Three days of work for a one-line change — and this is the process functioning
correctly, not bureaucracy. The alternative is not knowing.

The lesson for design: **architect so that most changes cannot affect the safety
path**, because the analysis cost is per change and it compounds. A team that
puts the safety overlay on a dedicated hardware plane with a guaranteed budget
pays once. A team that shares a GPU queue pays every release.
</Scenario>

## What an Android engineer should actually know

You will not write the safety case. These five things will still affect you:

**Know whether your feature is in the safety path.** Usually it is not, and
knowing that with confidence is what lets you move quickly.

**Never route safety-relevant data through Android** to save an interface. It
pulls Android into the argument and the cost is enormous.

**Respect the display layering.** The safety plane is above yours by design. Do
not design layouts that assume otherwise.

**Understand your resource budget is fixed.** Fixed cores and memory are what
buys freedom from interference. You cannot borrow.

**Take degraded modes seriously.** What your UI shows when a property goes stale
or a service dies is often a safety-adjacent requirement, even when your
component is QM. Showing a plausible stale value is worse than showing nothing.

<Recap>
- Functional safety asks only: **can a malfunction here cause harm, and what
  prevents it?**
- <T>ASIL</T> comes from **severity × exposure × controllability**, and it applies
  to a **hazard**, not to a box — which is what makes decomposition possible.
- One display can carry items from QM to ASIL C; build to the highest requirement
  only where it applies.
- **Consolidation removes free independence.** Every claim must now be argued:
  CPU, memory, peripherals, interrupts, power, thermal, time.
- A certified hypervisor covers only the configuration in its **safety manual**.
- The standard pattern: **make the safety function small**, isolate it, feed it
  independently, render it separately, watchdog it, and keep Android out.
- **Updates mean the safety case must be maintained**, per release, with impact
  analysis and traceability.
- Architect so that most changes **cannot** touch the safety path — the analysis
  cost is per change.
</Recap>
