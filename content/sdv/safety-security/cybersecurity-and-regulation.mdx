Security in automotive changed character around 2022, and not because the
technology changed. It changed because it became a condition of being allowed to
sell.

## What R155 and R156 actually do

<Plain>
<T>UNECE R155</T> requires a manufacturer to have a certified **Cybersecurity
Management System** — a demonstrable organisational process for identifying,
assessing and responding to cyber risk across a vehicle's whole lifecycle.

R156 requires a certified **Software Update Management System** — a demonstrable
process for how software gets changed on vehicles in the field.

Both are **type approval** requirements. Without them a vehicle type cannot be
approved for sale in the EU, UK, Japan, Korea and a growing list of others.

The shift is subtle and total. Previously, security was something a responsible
manufacturer did well and a careless one did badly, with the market as the only
referee. Now the absence of a process is a reason a vehicle cannot be sold.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="From a claim to an inspection">
A restaurant can say it takes hygiene seriously, and mean it. An inspector arriving to check
the temperature logs, the cleaning schedule and the training records is a
different relationship.

The food might have been fine before. The difference is that the process now has
to exist, be written down, and survive somebody looking at it.
</Analogy>

## What a CSMS requires in practice

The regulation is about process, so it lands as obligations on how you work.

| Requirement | What it means day to day |
|---|---|
| **Risk assessment** | A threat analysis (TARA) per system, kept current |
| **Risk treatment** | Mitigations traced to identified threats |
| **Verification** | Evidence that mitigations work — testing, pen testing |
| **Monitoring** | Watching for new vulnerabilities affecting the fleet |
| **Incident response** | A defined path from report to fix to deployment |
| **Supplier management** | Suppliers held to the same standard, contractually |
| **Lifecycle coverage** | From concept through production to end of support |

<Plain>
The two that most change engineering practice are **monitoring** and
**supplier management**.

Monitoring means somebody must be able to answer "does this new CVE affect our
vehicles?" — quickly, and for software shipped years ago. That is what makes an
<T>SBOM</T> a requirement rather than a nice-to-have.

Supplier management means the security posture of every component you did not
write becomes your problem. Most of a vehicle's software comes from suppliers,
and the OEM holds the type approval.
</Plain>

## Why the SBOM is the load-bearing artefact

<Scenario title="A vulnerability lands on a Friday afternoon">
A critical remote-code-execution vulnerability is announced in a widely used
compression library.

**Without an SBOM.** Email every supplier. Wait. Some reply within a day, some
within three weeks, some ask what version they shipped and go looking. Meanwhile
nobody can say whether the fleet is exposed. Six weeks later a partial answer
exists, and confidence in it is low.

**With an SBOM.** Query the build artefacts:

```bash
# which shipped images contain the affected library, at an affected version?
sbom-query --component "libcompress" --version-range "<2.4.1" \
           --releases "2023-*,2024-*,2025-*"
```

Answer in minutes: four images, two model years, roughly 180,000 vehicles.
Response planning starts the same afternoon.

The difference is not diligence. It is having built the inventory automatically
at the time the software was made, when it cost nothing, rather than trying to
reconstruct it under pressure when it costs everything.
</Scenario>

<Plain>
This is why CI pipelines are expected to emit an SBOM per build, why it is stored
alongside the artefact, and why "which version of what is in this image" must be
a query rather than an investigation.

If you take one operational point from this topic, it is that one.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
An SBOM covering only your own build is incomplete. Supplier binaries are the
majority of a vehicle's software, and the components inside them are exactly
where unpatched libraries hide.

Getting SBOMs from suppliers is a contractual and commercial problem more than a
technical one, and it is the part programmes underestimate.
</Gotcha>

## From report to fixed fleet

The path a vulnerability takes, and where it gets stuck.

```
  report arrives                     (researcher, supplier, CVE feed,
        │                             internal testing, bug bounty)
        ▼
  triage: are we affected?           ← SBOM makes this minutes, not weeks
        │
        ▼
  assess: exploitable here?          ← reachable? behind the gateway?
        │                              what does exploitation achieve?
        ▼
  fix + verify                       ← including a regression test
        │
        ▼
  safety impact analysis             ← does this touch the safety case?
        │
        ▼
  release + staged rollout           ← R156 evidence produced here
        │
        ▼
  confirm fleet coverage             ← who has not updated, and why?
```

<Plain>
Two stages absorb more time than people expect.

**Assessment.** Not every vulnerability in a component you ship is exploitable in
your configuration. A flaw in a parser you never feed untrusted data to is real
but not urgent. Getting this judgement right is what stops a team drowning in
low-severity noise — and getting it wrong in the optimistic direction is how
incidents happen.

**Fleet coverage confirmation.** Shipping the fix is not the end. Vehicles that
are offline, out of storage, or whose owners keep declining the update remain
vulnerable, sometimes for a very long time. The regulation asks what you do about
that, and "we published an update" is not a complete answer.
</Plain>

## Coordinated disclosure

<Plain>
Manufacturers now generally run a coordinated vulnerability disclosure process —
a published contact point, a commitment to respond, and an agreed timeline before
the researcher publishes.

This is a change in posture worth noting. The industry's earlier instinct toward
legal threats produced worse outcomes: researchers published without notice, and
fixes arrived after the exploit was public.

For an engineer, the practical consequence is that a report may arrive from
outside and will have a clock attached. The process for handling it needs to exist
before the first one lands.
</Plain>

## The end-of-support question

<Plain>
This is the genuinely unresolved part, and it is worth thinking about.

A vehicle lasts fifteen to twenty years. Software support does not. So:

- How long will security updates be provided? Some manufacturers now state this;
  many do not.
- What happens to a ten-year-old vehicle with an unpatched connected component?
- Is disabling connectivity an acceptable end state, when features the customer
  paid for depend on it?
- Who is responsible for a vehicle whose supplier no longer exists?

There is no settled industry answer. Regulators are looking at it, consumer
groups are pressing, and the first vehicles sold under R155 are still young.

Expect this to become a defined obligation within a few years. Designs that
assume indefinite cloud dependencies are accumulating a liability.
</Plain>

## What this means for you

**Your dependencies are a long-term commitment.** Adding a library is signing up
to track its vulnerabilities for the life of every vehicle it ships in. Prefer
fewer, well-maintained dependencies. This is a much stronger argument in
automotive than in web development.

**Your build must produce an inventory.** If your component's dependencies are
not in the SBOM, your component is a blind spot during the next incident.

**Write down what your component trusts.** During assessment, someone will ask
whether a vulnerable parser can be reached by untrusted input. A design note
answering that saves days.

**Report suspicious behaviour.** A crash on malformed input, an unexpected
network connection, a permission that seems too broad. Engineers noticing things
is a real part of the monitoring obligation.

**Expect security work to be scheduled, not heroic.** Patch cycles, dependency
updates and pen-test remediation are now planned work with regulatory deadlines.
A team treating them as interruptions will miss them.

<Recap>
- **R155 (cybersecurity) and R156 (software updates)** are type-approval
  requirements — without a certified management system, the vehicle cannot be
  sold.
- A CSMS requires risk assessment, treatment, verification, **monitoring**,
  incident response, **supplier management**, and full lifecycle coverage.
- The **SBOM** is the load-bearing artefact: it turns "are we affected?" from
  weeks of archaeology into a query.
- Supplier binaries dominate a vehicle's software, so **supplier SBOMs** are
  essential and are mostly a contractual problem.
- The response path stalls at **assessment** (is it exploitable here?) and at
  **fleet coverage** (who still has not updated?).
- **Coordinated disclosure** is now normal, and reports arrive with a clock.
- **End of support is unresolved** — assume it will become a defined obligation
  and avoid open-ended cloud dependencies.
- For you: dependencies are long-term commitments, your build must emit an
  inventory, document what your component trusts, report anomalies, and expect
  security work on the schedule.
</Recap>
