Consolidating the cluster with infotainment saves real money. The price is that
the isolation which used to be obvious — they were separate boxes — now has to be
argued for in a document.

This page is about what that document contains and what it demands of your work.

## What the cluster owes the driver

Two distinct obligations, often conflated:

**Regulatory.** Specific telltales must be a specific colour and symbol, and must
appear when their condition occurs. These come from vehicle regulations, not from
<T>ASIL</T> ratings, and they are checked at homologation.

**Functional safety.** The rated content must be **correct** — a speedometer that
displays 50 when the vehicle is doing 70 is a safety failure, not a cosmetic one.
This is <T>ISO 26262</T> territory, with an ASIL rating attached.

<Plain>
A useful way to hold them apart: regulation is mostly about **presence and
appearance**; functional safety is mostly about **correctness and availability**.

A cluster can satisfy one and fail the other.
</Plain>

## The three failure modes a safety case must address

For any displayed value, the analysis asks what happens if it is:

**Absent.** The speedometer shows nothing. Usually detectable by the driver, and
usually the least dangerous of the three.

**Wrong.** The speedometer shows 50 when the vehicle is doing 70. **This is the
dangerous one**, because the driver has no way to know and will act on it.

**Stale.** The speedometer shows a value from four seconds ago. Dangerous for the
same reason as wrong, and harder to detect from inside the system.

<Scenario title="Why 'fail visibly' is a design requirement">
A cluster receives speed over a link that stops.

**Bad design:** keep displaying the last value. The driver sees a plausible
number and has no reason to doubt it.

**Good design:** the value is dashed out, or the gauge greys, within a defined
timeout. The driver immediately knows the vehicle is not telling them their
speed.

Nobody enjoys designing a UI that visibly breaks. But **absent is much safer than
wrong**, and a safety case will ask you to demonstrate which one you produce.

This is the single most transferable idea in this topic: every rated value needs
a defined staleness timeout and a visible degraded state.
</Scenario>

## Freedom from interference, concretely

When rated and unrated software share hardware, <T>freedom from interference</T>
must be demonstrated in three dimensions.

**Memory.** The infotainment guest cannot read or write the safety guest's
memory. Evidence: hypervisor configuration, hardware memory protection settings,
and tests attempting violations.

**Timing.** The infotainment guest cannot delay the safety guest. Evidence: core
allocation, scheduling configuration, and measurement under adversarial load.

**Exchange of information.** A corrupted or malicious message from the
infotainment guest cannot cause incorrect behaviour in the safety guest.
Evidence: the receiver validates everything, bounds the channel, and has a
defined fallback.

<Plain>
The third is where designs most often fall short, because the first two are
configuration and the third is discipline.

The rule that satisfies it: **the safety side treats the infotainment side as
untrusted input**, exactly as if it came from the internet. Validate length,
validate version, validate ranges, reject anything unexpected, and have a
fallback for silence.
</Plain>

## The evidence a safety case actually wants

Not code quality opinions — artefacts:

| Evidence | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Hazard analysis | Every displayed item, its failure modes, and the resulting risk |
| Safety requirements | Derived from the hazards, traceable both ways |
| Architecture rationale | Why this partitioning satisfies those requirements |
| Isolation evidence | Configuration plus tests attempting to violate it |
| Timing evidence | Measured worst-case under adversarial load, not typical load |
| Tool qualification | Confidence in the compiler, hypervisor and generators used |
| Verification results | Tests traceable back to requirements |

<Plain>
The pattern to notice: almost all of it is **traceability**. A hazard leads to a
requirement leads to a design decision leads to a test result.

Which means the practical advice for an engineer is unglamorous: when you make a
safety-relevant decision, **write down why, in a place the safety engineer can
reference.** Reconstructing rationale eighteen months later is expensive and
sometimes impossible.
</Plain>

## Requirements that keep landing on the wrong system

These arrive on infotainment teams regularly, and all of them belong elsewhere:

- "The head unit shall display the brake warning lamp."
- "The infotainment system shall alert the driver when adaptive cruise
  disengages."
- "Android shall guarantee the speed display updates within 100 ms."
- "The head unit shall place the emergency call."
- Any availability figure Android cannot meet.

<Plain>
The correct response is not to attempt it and hope. It is to raise it as an
architectural finding, name the system that should own it, and involve the safety
engineer.

Doing that in week one is a five-minute conversation. Doing it during
homologation is a programme problem, and by then somebody has built the thing.

In my experience safety engineers are pleased to be asked — the requirement
usually landed on the wrong component through a documentation path, not a
decision.
</Plain>

## Testing the safety argument

Three tests worth running early and repeatedly:

```bash title="1. Kill the unrated guest"
# From the infotainment guest, on a development build
adb shell stop
# Cluster must be completely unaffected: gauges, telltales, no flicker
adb shell start
```

```text title="2. Load the shared resources adversarially"
Run a GPU benchmark, saturate memory bandwidth, and flood the inter-guest
channel — simultaneously. Measure cluster frame timing throughout.
```

```text title="3. Corrupt the channel"
Send malformed messages: wrong version, oversized, truncated, wrong field types,
absurd values. The safety guest must reject every one and keep working.
```

<Plain>
Test 3 is the one most often skipped and the one a reviewer will ask about. It is
also easy to automate, which makes it a good candidate for the pipeline.
</Plain>

<Recap>
- Two separate obligations: **regulatory** (presence and appearance) and
  **functional safety** (correctness and availability).
- Three failure modes: **absent, wrong, stale**. Wrong and stale are the dangerous
  ones because the driver cannot detect them.
- **Absent is safer than wrong** — every rated value needs a staleness timeout and
  a visible degraded state.
- **Freedom from interference** must be shown in memory, timing *and* information
  exchange. The third requires the safety side to treat the other as untrusted.
- A safety case wants **traceable artefacts**, so write down rationale as you go.
- Requirements land on the wrong system constantly. **Escalate early — it is a
  five-minute conversation in week one.**
</Recap>
