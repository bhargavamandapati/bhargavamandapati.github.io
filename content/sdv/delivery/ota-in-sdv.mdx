<T>OTA</T> is the mechanism the whole software-defined vehicle idea rests on. If
you cannot change software after the sale, nothing else in this section matters.

You already know Android's update mechanism. What is worth understanding is
everything around it, because updating a head unit and updating a vehicle are
different problems.

## Three scopes, increasing in difficulty

<Plain>
**Infotainment-only.** Apps and the head-unit stack. Roughly the phone problem:
familiar tooling, contained blast radius, and if it fails the driver loses media
rather than transport.

**Cockpit including the cluster.** Now a failed update can leave a car without a
speedometer. Regulated content is involved. The bar rises sharply.

**Full vehicle.** Powertrain, braking, ADAS, body controllers, zone controllers.
A bad update here can immobilise a vehicle or make it unsafe. This is where the
process becomes genuinely heavy, and rightly so.

Most programmes start at the first and work outward over several model years.
When someone says "we do OTA", ask which scope.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="Updating a phone versus rewiring a house">
Updating an app is routine. Worst case, uninstall it.

Updating the firmware of the house's consumer unit while people are inside is a
different activity. You would want the power off, someone qualified present, a
tested rollback, and a way to know it worked before anyone flips a switch.

Vehicle OTA is closer to the second, which is why the preconditions are so
strict.
</Analogy>

## The preconditions nobody mentions

An update does not simply arrive. A long list of conditions must hold first, and
most real-world OTA frustration comes from these rather than from the download.

| Condition | Why |
|---|---|
| **Parked** | You cannot reboot an ECU at 100 km/h |
| **Not about to be driven** | An interrupted install is the worst case |
| **Sufficient battery** | A flash that browns out mid-write can brick an ECU |
| **Charging, for large updates** | On an EV, a big flash is a real energy draw |
| **Connectivity** | Cellular data costs money and coverage is uneven |
| **User consent** | Legally required in most markets, and expected everywhere |
| **Compatible configuration** | This hardware revision, these dependent versions |
| **No pending fault** | Do not update a car that is already unhappy |

<Scenario title="Why the update did not install">
A customer complains they have been prompted for weeks and it never happens.

The trace shows: the vehicle is parked at an office for eight hours a day with
poor cellular signal indoors, driven home, parked on a street with no charging,
and the state of charge is usually below the 40% threshold by evening. Consent
was given weeks ago.

Nothing is broken. The preconditions simply never coincided.

The fixes are product decisions, not code: allow download over the owner's
home Wi-Fi, separate the download threshold from the install threshold, and let
the customer schedule an install window explicitly.

Most "OTA is unreliable" complaints look like this on inspection.
</Scenario>

## The A/B pattern, and its limits

Android's <T>A/B partitions</T> approach is the right mental model, and the wider
vehicle uses the same idea where it can.

```
   ┌─────────── slot A (running) ───────────┐
   │  system, vendor, product               │   ← in use, untouched
   └────────────────────────────────────────┘
   ┌─────────── slot B (idle) ──────────────┐
   │  new image written here while driving  │   ← written in the background
   └────────────────────────────────────────┘
                    │
              reboot into B
                    │
        ┌───────────┴────────────┐
        │ boots and marks good?  │
        └───────┬────────────┬───┘
              yes            no
               │              │
         B becomes active   fall back to A automatically
```

<Plain>
The property that matters is that **the running system is never modified**. A
failure at any point before the switch leaves the vehicle exactly as it was.

That is why <T>Garage Mode</T> and background installs are safe to do while the
vehicle is in use: nothing the customer depends on is being touched.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
A/B costs storage — roughly double for the partitions it covers. On a
cost-sensitive programme this is a real argument, and some ECUs deep in the
vehicle do **not** get A/B for that reason.

Those are the ones where a failed update is unrecoverable without a workshop
visit, and they are exactly why full-vehicle OTA is treated so much more
cautiously than infotainment OTA.
</Gotcha>

## Orchestration across many ECUs

A vehicle update is rarely one image. It is a campaign across components with
dependencies between them.

<Plain>
A typical campaign has to answer:

- **Order.** The gateway may need updating before the ECUs behind it.
- **Atomicity.** If ECU 4 of 7 fails, what state is the vehicle in? Can it be
  driven? Is there a defined partial-success state, or must everything roll
  back?
- **Compatibility.** Version 5 of the cluster may require version 3 or later of
  the body controller. The campaign must know this and refuse mismatches.
- **Time budget.** The whole sequence must fit in a window the customer accepts.
  Forty minutes is tolerable. Three hours is not.
- **Interruption.** Someone will get in and try to drive. The system must handle
  that safely at every point.

This orchestration logic — not the download, not the flash — is where most of the
engineering effort in vehicle OTA actually goes.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="A campaign that had to be designed backwards">
A programme wants to ship a new charging feature. It touches the charge
controller, the gateway's routing rules, and the cluster UI.

First attempt: update all three in one campaign. During dry runs, a failure on
the charge controller left a vehicle whose gateway expected the new message
format and whose controller sent the old one. The vehicle could drive but could
not charge — the worst possible outcome for a charging feature.

The redesign: make the gateway accept **both** message formats, ship that first
as its own campaign, and only then update the controller and the cluster.

The lesson generalises. **Make the receiving side tolerant before you change the
sending side.** It is the same discipline as a backwards-compatible schema
migration, with a much less forgiving rollback.
</Scenario>

## Security is not optional here

An update channel is a way to put code on a vehicle. Anyone who compromises it
owns the fleet.

<Plain>
The baseline expectations:

- **Signed images**, verified by the vehicle before installation, with keys in
  hardware where possible.
- **Verified boot** so a tampered image does not run even if it is written.
- **Rollback protection** so an attacker cannot install an older image with a
  known vulnerability.
- **Authenticated servers**, so a vehicle cannot be pointed at an attacker's
  update endpoint.
- **Uptane** or equivalent — a framework designed specifically for this threat
  model, with separate roles and keys so compromising one server is not enough.

<T>UNECE R155</T> and R156 make much of this a type-approval requirement rather
than a recommendation. A vehicle without a certified update management system
cannot be sold in the markets that have adopted them.
</Plain>

## Staged rollout, and why it is different here

<Plain>
The web pattern — deploy to 1%, watch the dashboards, ramp up — applies, with two
adjustments.

**The observation window is longer.** Vehicles are driven intermittently. A fault
that appears on the third cold start after an update takes days to surface, not
minutes. Ramping on a one-hour signal will mislead you.

**Stopping the rollout does not recall it.** Halting the campaign prevents *new*
vehicles from updating. Vehicles already updated stay updated until another
campaign reaches them, and some of those will be offline for weeks.

So the ramp is deliberately slow: internal fleet, then employees, then a small
customer cohort in one market, then wider, over weeks. And the criteria for
proceeding must include signals that take days to appear — not just install
success rate.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
Install success rate is a seductive and insufficient metric. An update can
install perfectly and still be a failure: battery drain, a fault light, a feature
that no longer works in cold weather.

Rollout gates should include fault-code rates, customer contact volume, and
feature-level telemetry — not just "did the flash complete".
</Gotcha>

## What this means for your Android work

**Assume your code will be replaced.** Design for it. Persistent state must
survive an update, and must tolerate being read by a newer version.

**Assume the versions around you will differ.** During a staged rollout, your app
may run against an older VHAL or an older vehicle service. Handle absent
properties and unknown values rather than assuming a matched set.

**Keep the update path small.** Anything that shrinks the image — shared
libraries, avoiding duplicated assets, delta-friendly layouts — is cellular data
the customer does not pay for and install time they do not wait through.

**Never assume connectivity.** Features that break without a network will break,
and in a car that is a common state rather than an edge case.

**Test the upgrade, not just the install.** The interesting bugs are in migration
from the previous version with real accumulated state — not in a clean flash.

<Recap>
- OTA is what makes the SDV possible; scope matters — **infotainment, cockpit,
  or full vehicle** are three very different problems.
- Most OTA frustration comes from **preconditions** (parked, charged, connected,
  consented, compatible) rather than from the update itself.
- **A/B partitions** work because the running system is never modified, so any
  failure before the switch is harmless — but they cost storage, and not every
  ECU gets them.
- The real engineering is **campaign orchestration**: order, atomicity,
  compatibility, time budget, interruption.
- Make the **receiving side tolerant before changing the sending side**.
- Security is a type-approval requirement: signed images, verified boot, rollback
  protection, authenticated servers, Uptane, R155/R156.
- **Staged rollout is slower here** — the observation window is days, and stopping
  a campaign does not recall what already shipped.
- For your code: expect replacement, expect version skew, keep images small,
  never assume connectivity, and test the **upgrade** path.
</Recap>
