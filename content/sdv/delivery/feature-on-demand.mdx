<T>Feature on demand</T> is the commercial reason many SDV programmes are funded.
It is also the part of the SDV story that has generated the most customer anger,
and the difference between the two outcomes is worth understanding precisely.

## The idea

<Plain>
Build one hardware specification. Sell capability separately — at purchase,
later, as a subscription, or as a trial.

The manufacturing argument is genuinely strong. Fitting every vehicle identically
removes variant complexity from a factory, from the supply chain, and from the
service network. One wiring harness instead of nine. One spare part instead of
four.

The commercial argument is that revenue continues after the sale, and that a
customer who did not want a feature in year one can buy it in year three.
</Plain>

## Where it works, and where it does not

This is the crux, and it is not subtle once stated.

| Reception | Kind of feature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Accepted** | Ongoing service with ongoing cost | Live traffic, connected navigation, remote services, hotspot data |
| **Accepted** | Genuinely new capability delivered later | A driver-assist function that did not exist at build |
| **Accepted** | Temporary or occasional use | Trailer-towing mode for one weekend, a trial before buying |
| **Rejected** | Hardware present, artificially disabled | Heated seats you can feel under you, unlocked by subscription |
| **Rejected** | Capability the customer believes they bought | Full battery range, already-fitted acceleration |

<Plain>
The pattern behind the line: customers accept paying for something that
**costs the manufacturer money to keep providing**, and reject paying repeatedly
for something **already installed in the car they own**.

Heated seats became the notorious case because the hardware is physically present
and costs nothing to run. Charging monthly for it reads as withholding, and
customers said so loudly enough that the plan was withdrawn.

Live traffic, by contrast, requires servers and data feeds indefinitely. Almost
nobody objects to paying for that.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="A gym membership versus a locked room in your house">
Paying monthly for a gym is normal — the gym has staff, equipment and a building,
and stops costing you when you stop going.

Paying monthly to unlock a room in the house you bought is not the same
transaction, however the contract is written. The room is already yours; the
lock is the product.

Every FoD proposal should be tested against that distinction before engineering
starts.
</Analogy>

<Gotcha>
This is a genuinely useful thing to raise in a design review, and it is a
technical point as well as an ethical one. Features on the wrong side of the line
generate customer anger, press coverage, regulatory attention, and — very
reliably — an aftermarket unlocking industry that becomes your security problem.

Engineering effort spent gating a feature customers believe they own is effort
spent building something you will later be asked to remove.
</Gotcha>

## How entitlement actually works

The mechanism is the same regardless of the feature.

```
  purchase (app, web, in-car store)
            │
            ▼
   ┌──────────────────┐
   │ entitlement      │   the authority: which VIN owns what, until when
   │ service (cloud)  │
   └────────┬─────────┘
            │ signed entitlement token, bound to VIN, with expiry
            ▼
   ┌──────────────────┐
   │ vehicle          │   verifies signature, stores securely,
   │ entitlement mgr  │   re-validates when connectivity allows
   └────────┬─────────┘
            │
   ┌────────┴──────────┬──────────────────┐
   ▼                   ▼                  ▼
 feature flag      capability        UI visibility
 in the ECU        in the VHAL       in the app
 that performs     that permits      that presents
 the function      the write         the control
```

<Plain>
Three properties do the real work.

**Signed and VIN-bound.** A token lifted from one vehicle must not work in
another. Signature verification happens on the vehicle, against a key it holds in
hardware.

**Enforced at the point of action, not in the UI.** Hiding a button is
presentation. The check that matters is in the component that actually performs
the function.

**Works offline.** A vehicle in an underground car park must still know what it is
entitled to. Tokens are cached with an expiry long enough to survive realistic
offline periods, and re-validated opportunistically.
</Plain>

<Gotcha>
Enforcing entitlement only in the app layer is the classic mistake. Someone will
send the underlying intent, call the API directly, or install a modified APK.

The rule is the same as any authorisation design: **the layer that performs the
action is the layer that must check.** In practice that means the VHAL
implementation or the vehicle-side service, not the Activity.
</Gotcha>

## What this forces on the engineering

FoD is not a feature you add at the end. It changes how the whole build is
structured.

**One image, many configurations.** You can no longer branch the build per trim,
because every vehicle ships the same software. Differences become runtime state.
This is a good discipline — it collapses the variant matrix that makes automotive
CI slow — but it has to be designed in.

**Every feature needs a defined off state.** Not "the button is hidden" but what
the whole system does: what the cluster shows, what a voice command answers, what
happens if an entitlement expires mid-drive. Those are product decisions that
need answers before code.

**Graceful expiry.** A subscription ending while the vehicle is moving must not
change vehicle behaviour abruptly. The usual rule is that anything affecting
driving continues until the vehicle is next parked.

**Testing multiplies.** Every FoD feature at least doubles its own test matrix:
entitled, not entitled, expired, expiring during use, offline with a cached
token, offline with an expired token, and transferred to a new owner.

**Ownership transfer.** When the vehicle is sold, entitlements must move or end
predictably. Getting this wrong produces a second owner who paid for a car whose
features silently stopped, which is a support and reputation problem.

<Scenario title="Trailer mode, designed properly">
An OEM offers a towing package purchasable for a weekend. It changes stability
control tuning, adds a trailer view to the cluster, and alters range estimation.

**Entitlement.** Purchased in the app, token issued for 72 hours bound to the VIN,
delivered next time the vehicle is online.

**Enforcement.** The stability-control ECU checks entitlement itself before
applying trailer tuning — it does not trust a message from infotainment. The
cluster view and range model check separately.

**Off state.** Without entitlement, the trailer view is absent from the cluster
menu entirely rather than shown greyed out, because a greyed control the customer
cannot use is an advertisement, not a UI.

**Expiry.** At 72 hours, if the vehicle is moving with a trailer connected,
tuning **stays active** until the next park. Safety is not a subscription.

**Offline.** Token cached; a vehicle out of coverage for the whole weekend still
works.

**Test matrix.** Entitled towing, unentitled towing, expiry while parked, expiry
while towing, offline purchase delivery, and purchase while already towing.

That is one modest feature, and the design work is substantial. Multiply by a
catalogue and you see why FoD is an architecture decision rather than a product
decision.
</Scenario>

## Doing it without antagonising customers

Patterns that have worked in practice:

**Charge for ongoing cost, not for unlocking.** The clearest test of whether a
feature belongs in the catalogue.

**Offer permanent purchase alongside subscription.** Customers object far less
when a subscription is a choice rather than the only route.

**Be explicit at the point of sale.** "This vehicle includes the hardware;
this function requires a subscription" printed clearly is a different transaction
from a discovery in year two.

**Let trials be genuine.** A trial that converts silently into a charge is the
fastest way to lose the argument.

**Never gate safety.** Emergency braking, stability control, warning telltales.
Some markets are moving to make this explicit in regulation; the reputational
cost already makes it settled.

**Make ownership transfer work well.** It is the cheapest goodwill available and
the most commonly neglected.

<Recap>
- FoD means **one hardware build, capability sold separately** — the
  manufacturing argument for this is genuinely strong.
- Customers accept paying for **ongoing cost** (traffic, connectivity, new
  capability, temporary use) and reject paying for **unlocking hardware they
  already own**.
- Entitlement is a **signed, VIN-bound token** verified on the vehicle, cached to
  work offline, and re-validated when possible.
- Enforcement must live in the **component that performs the action**, never only
  in the UI.
- FoD forces one image with runtime configuration, a defined off state per
  feature, graceful expiry, ownership transfer, and a much larger test matrix.
- **Safety-relevant behaviour is never gated**, and an entitlement expiring
  mid-drive must not change how the vehicle drives.
- The catalogue test is simple: does this cost the manufacturer money to keep
  providing?
</Recap>
