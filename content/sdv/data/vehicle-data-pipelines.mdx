"Vehicles generate terabytes of data" appears in every SDV presentation. It is
true and almost entirely misleading, because the interesting question is not how
much is generated but how little can usefully leave.

## The numbers that shape everything

<Plain>
A modern vehicle's internal buses carry on the order of **tens of megabytes per
second** of signal traffic. Add camera and radar feeds and it reaches gigabytes
per second internally.

A connected vehicle's realistic cellular budget is on the order of **tens to
hundreds of megabytes per month** — because someone pays for every byte, coverage
is uneven, and the customer's data plan may be involved.

That is a gap of roughly six orders of magnitude.
</Plain>

<Analogy title="A fire hose and a drinking straw">
The vehicle produces at fire-hose rate. The link to the outside is a straw.

So the entire design problem is not transport. It is **deciding what deserves to
go through the straw**, and doing that decision-making on the vehicle.

Any data architecture that treats the network as the hard part has misread the
problem.
</Analogy>

### Collection

Signals come off the buses, out of the <T>data broker</T>, and from application
telemetry. At this point everything exists and nothing has been decided.

### On-vehicle filtering

<Plain>
This is where the volume problem is solved, and it is the most important stage.
Four techniques, in rough order of how much they save:

**Event triggering.** Send nothing until something happens. Ninety-nine percent
of driving is unremarkable; a hard-braking event is not.

**Aggregation.** Do not send 100 Hz wheel speed. Send a summary per minute:
mean, max, and the count of samples outside a band.

**Change-based reporting.** Send a value only when it moves by more than a
threshold. Cabin temperature does not need reporting at 10 Hz.

**Local computation.** Compute the answer on the vehicle and send the answer. If
the question is "how often does the driver override lane keeping", the vehicle
can count, and send an integer per day rather than a stream.
</Plain>

<Scenario title="One signal, four designs">
The question: does a new suspension setting reduce harsh events?

**Naive.** Stream vertical acceleration at 100 Hz. That is roughly 400 bytes/s,
about 1 GB per vehicle per month. Across a fleet of 100,000 vehicles it is
100 petabytes per month and a cellular bill nobody will sign.

**Event-triggered.** Send a 5-second window around each event above a threshold.
Perhaps 30 events per month × 2 KB = 60 KB per vehicle. A factor of ~17,000
saved, and the answer is just as good.

**Aggregated.** Send a daily histogram of event magnitudes. About 200 bytes per
vehicle per day. Loses the waveform, keeps the distribution — sufficient if you
only need the trend.

**Computed locally.** Send one number per day: harsh-event count. 8 bytes.
Sufficient if the question really is only "more or fewer".

All four answer a version of the question. The engineering skill is knowing
which version you actually need — and the default mistake is choosing a design
that answers questions nobody has asked yet, at a cost somebody will notice.
</Scenario>

### Buffering

<Plain>
Vehicles go offline constantly — car parks, tunnels, rural roads, foreign
markets, and simply being switched off for a week.

So the vehicle stores what it intends to send, and forwards when it can. Which
forces three decisions:

- **How much storage** may telemetry use? Flash is finite and has write
  endurance.
- **What is dropped first** when the buffer fills? Almost always: keep fault
  data, drop routine aggregates.
- **What survives a power cycle?** Anything only in RAM is gone at every
  ignition-off.

A pipeline that assumes connectivity will silently lose exactly the data from
the vehicles that are most interesting — the ones in unusual conditions.
</Plain>

### Transport and ingestion

Batched, compressed, authenticated, usually MQTT or HTTPS. The cloud side is
ordinary data engineering: ingest, validate, store, and make queryable.

<Gotcha>
Schema evolution is the part that bites. Vehicles in the field run software from
several model years, all emitting slightly different payloads for years.

Version every message, never reuse a field name with a new meaning, and make the
ingestion side tolerant of both older and newer shapes than it expects. A schema
change that assumes the whole fleet updates together is a schema change that will
lose data.
</Gotcha>

## The categories of data, and their different rules

Not all vehicle data is the same kind of thing, and treating it uniformly is a
mistake.

| Category | Example | Volume | Sensitivity | Typically |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Diagnostic** | Fault codes, ECU health | Low | Low | Always collected |
| **Operational** | Speed, energy use, temperatures | Medium | Medium | Aggregated |
| **Behavioural** | Braking style, feature usage | Medium | **High** | Consent required |
| **Location** | GNSS trace | Medium | **Very high** | Consent, minimised |
| **Media** | Camera, microphone | Enormous | **Very high** | Almost never leaves |
| **Application** | Crash logs, UI telemetry | Low | Medium | Standard app rules |

<Plain>
Two rows deserve attention.

**Location** is special because a trace is identifying even without a name
attached. Where a vehicle sleeps every night is a home address. Regulators treat
it accordingly, and so should you.

**Media** almost never leaves the vehicle, and when it does — a crash snapshot,
an ADAS training clip — it is a deliberate, consented, tightly scoped exception,
not a pipeline.
</Plain>

## What this means for cockpit engineering

**Your app telemetry is part of a shared budget.** An analytics SDK sending
events per interaction is trivial on a phone and can be significant across a
fleet on metered cellular. Batch, aggregate, and consider whether each event
earns its bytes.

**Aggregate before sending, not after.** The instinct from server work — collect
everything, decide later — inverts here. Deciding later means paying to transmit
data you will discard.

**Design for offline as the normal case.** Not an error path. Queue, persist
across reboot, and define what gets dropped when the queue is full.

**Never log location or personal data casually.** A debug log with GPS
coordinates that syncs to a backend is a compliance incident, and the
investigation will find it.

**Know which consent your feature depends on.** If it needs behavioural data,
what happens for a customer who declined? "It silently does nothing" is not an
acceptable answer.

<Scenario title="A well-behaved feature telemetry design">
A team wants to know whether a redesigned media browser is easier to use.

**Wrong:** log every tap with a timestamp and screen name; upload continuously.
Roughly 2 KB per drive per vehicle, always on, and it is behavioural data
requiring consent for a question that does not need per-tap detail.

**Right:** compute the metric on the vehicle — median taps to start playback, per
session. Accumulate a daily summary. Upload once, batched with everything else,
about 40 bytes. No individual interactions leave. No consent-gated category is
touched, because a distribution is not behaviour tied to a person.

The team gets a better answer — a distribution across the fleet rather than a pile
of events to process — for a thousandth of the data and none of the privacy
exposure.
</Scenario>

<Recap>
- A vehicle produces data at **fire-hose rate** and has a **drinking-straw** link
  out; the whole problem is deciding what deserves to be sent.
- On-vehicle filtering is the load-bearing stage: **event triggering,
  aggregation, change-based reporting, local computation**.
- **Buffer for offline** — persist across power cycles and define what is dropped
  first, or you will lose data from the most interesting vehicles.
- **Version every message** and tolerate old and new shapes; the fleet never
  updates together.
- Data categories have different rules — **location and media are in a class of
  their own**.
- For cockpit work: your telemetry shares a fleet budget, aggregate before
  sending, treat offline as normal, never log location casually, and know which
  consent your feature needs.
</Recap>
