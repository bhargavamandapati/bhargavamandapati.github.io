`data-governance-and-privacy` covered what a vehicle is legally allowed to
collect and how long it may keep it. This topic is the question that sits
on top of a compliant answer to that one: once data collection is lawful,
who else is allowed to use it, and under what commercial terms — because
"we may collect it" and "we may sell access to it" are two separate
decisions, made by different people, on different timelines.

<Plain>
Most of the SDV pitch to an OEM's board is not "the software updates
better." It is "the vehicle becomes a data asset" — insurance telemetry,
aggregated traffic and road-condition feeds, fleet analytics sold back to
fleet operators, driving-behaviour data licensed to insurers who price a
policy on it. None of that is a platform decision. All of it depends on the
platform having been built to make it possible, which is the part that
lands on an SDV engineer.
</Plain>

## Three shapes of "sharing," rarely distinguished carefully

| Shape | What actually moves | Typical buyer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-party analytics | Aggregated, usually on-device summarised | The OEM itself |
| B2B data licensing | A defined feed, per signal, per contract | Insurers, fleet operators, road authorities |
| Third-party app access | Live, scoped API access at request time | App developers, via a permission model |

The first is what most of `data-governance-and-privacy`'s checklist already
covers — aggregate on device, upload summaries. The second and third are
where an SDV platform has to make an explicit, durable design decision, because
they involve handing raw or near-raw access to a party the driver has never
directly interacted with.

## Why this is a platform decision, not a business one, underneath

A sales contract can promise a fleet operator "access to vehicle location
and driving behaviour." What the platform actually has to provide is a
*scoped, revocable, audited* channel that delivers exactly that and nothing
else — the contract's promises are worthless if the only implementation
available is "give them a key to the whole telemetry pipe." This is the
same permission-boundary discipline `vehicle property access modes` already
apply to an app on the vehicle; third-party data sharing is that same
discipline extended to a party that is not running code on the vehicle at
all.

<Analogy title="A tenant subletting one room, not handing over the house keys">
Licensing driving-behaviour data to an insurer should look like subletting
one room with its own lock — the insurer gets exactly what the lease says,
nothing else in the house is reachable through that key, and the lease can
be terminated without anyone re-keying the whole building. A platform that
can only grant "the whole telemetry pipe, tagged for this buyer" has built a
master key and is trusting the paperwork to behave like a lock.
</Analogy>

## What a data-sharing platform actually needs

**A consent and scope registry**, per driver, per data category, per buyer
— not a single opt-in toggle. A driver who consents to insurance telemetry
has not consented to that same feed being resold to a marketing analytics
firm, even if both requests originate from data the vehicle already
collects for its own operational purposes.

**Purpose binding**, enforced technically, not just contractually. A feed
scoped to "aggregate road-condition data" should be structurally incapable
of also carrying a VIN or a precise driver identity — the safest design
makes the wrong use impossible rather than merely prohibited.

**Revocation that actually reaches the buyer's pipeline**, not just the
vehicle's own upload job. If a driver withdraws consent, or a contract ends,
the platform needs a real mechanism to stop that specific feed — which
means every sharing arrangement needs its own identifiable channel from the
start, not a shared firehose filtered downstream by a promise.

<Scenario title="On a real programme">
An OEM signs a data-licensing deal with an insurer for aggregated
harsh-braking events, priced per vehicle-month. Engineering discovers the
existing telemetry pipeline has exactly one export path, already carrying
several other feeds tagged by internal metadata alone. Meeting the
contract's actual terms — the insurer sees only what they paid for, the OEM
can prove it in an audit, and the feed can be cut off independently of
every other consumer — requires a genuinely new, separately-scoped export
path, not a filter bolted onto the existing one. The lesson generalises: a
telemetry pipeline built for the OEM's own internal use is very rarely
already shaped correctly for external commercial sharing, and discovering
that after the contract is signed is expensive.
</Scenario>

## Where this intersects the rest of the curriculum

`edge-vs-cloud` already covers the infrastructure question of where data
lands; monetization adds a second axis to that decision — data destined for
external sale needs its aggregation and anonymisation boundary drawn
*before* it leaves vehicle-controlled infrastructure, not after, because
"we will filter it once it reaches our cloud" means the raw feed already
transited a boundary the contract may not have authorised. `zero trust in
the vehicle` applies here too: a third-party data buyer's pipeline is, from
the vehicle's perspective, exactly as untrusted as any other external
party, regardless of how much revenue the contract represents.

<Callout type="warning" title="A monetization plan is a data-governance plan with a price attached">
Every design question data-governance-and-privacy raises — what category is
this, who can withdraw consent, how is it minimised — applies with equal
force to a paying third party. The money changing hands does not relax the
requirement; if anything, a contract creates an auditable paper trail that
makes a governance gap far more expensive to discover late.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="The pure-OEM-monetization model is under active legal pressure">
Everything above describes the platform engineering an OEM controls
internally, but the assumption that the OEM decides who gets access is
itself contested and shifting. The **EU Data Act** — in force since 2024,
with most of its obligations applicable since September 2025 — grants
vehicle owners and users a right to access data their vehicle generates and
to direct that it be shared with third parties of their choosing, including
the OEM's own competitors and independent repairers. Combined with ongoing
right-to-repair pressure in other markets, this is not settled ground: a
platform built only around "the OEM licenses access on its own terms" may
need to support a driver-initiated sharing request it did not originate.
</Callout>

<Recap>
- Data monetization is a separate decision layered on top of lawful
  collection, not a consequence of it — "may collect" and "may sell access
  to" are different questions, usually decided by different people.
- First-party analytics, B2B licensing and third-party app access are three
  distinct shapes, each needing a different technical scoping mechanism, not
  one shared telemetry export.
- A durable sharing platform needs per-buyer, per-category consent tracking,
  technically enforced purpose binding, and revocation that reaches the
  actual downstream pipeline — not just the vehicle's own upload job.
- A telemetry pipeline built for internal use is rarely already shaped for
  external commercial sharing; discovering that after a contract is signed
  is an expensive way to find out.
</Recap>
