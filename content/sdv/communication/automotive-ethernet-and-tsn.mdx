Everything in this module rests on the network. Services, data brokers and
central compute all assume that moving data around the vehicle is fast and
predictable — which, on <T>CAN</T>, it is not.

## What CAN could and could not do

CAN has been the vehicle backbone since the 1980s, and it earned that position.
It is extremely robust, cheap, needs only two wires, and degrades gracefully.

Its limits, though, are structural:

| | CAN | What a modern vehicle needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Up to 1 Mbit/s | Camera and sensor data: hundreds of Mbit/s |
| Payload | 8 bytes per frame | Structured messages, sometimes kilobytes |
| Topology | Shared bus, everyone hears everything | Switched, point-to-point |
| Addressing | None — messages are numbered, not addressed | Named endpoints |
| Growth | Adding traffic loads the whole bus | Capacity that scales |

<Plain>
The 8-byte payload is the constraint people underestimate. A service call with a
few parameters and a structured response does not fit. Everything must be
fragmented and reassembled, which is why signal-oriented design was not really a
choice — it was what the wire allowed.
</Plain>

## Why ordinary Ethernet was not enough

Office Ethernet is fast, cheap and universal. Two things made it unusable in a
vehicle without modification.

**Cabling.** Standard Ethernet uses four twisted pairs and bulky connectors.
Automotive Ethernet (100BASE-T1, 1000BASE-T1) runs over **a single twisted pair**
with automotive connectors, tolerating vehicle temperature ranges and
electromagnetic conditions.

**Timing.** This is the important one.

<Plain>
Standard Ethernet makes no promise about *when* a packet arrives. Under load,
packets queue. Usually that is invisible — a web page arriving 40 ms later does
not matter.

In a vehicle it matters enormously. If a brake-related message can be delayed
behind a camera frame, the network is unusable for control, regardless of how
fast it is on average.

**Speed was never the hard part. Predictability was.**
</Plain>

## What TSN adds

<T>TSN</T> — Time-Sensitive Networking — is a set of IEEE standards that add
guarantees to Ethernet. The pieces that matter in a vehicle:

**Time synchronisation.** Every device shares a common clock, accurate to
microseconds. Everything else depends on this.

**Scheduled traffic.** The network runs on a repeating schedule with reserved
slots. A control message has a slot; nothing else may transmit during it.

**Frame preemption.** A large, low-priority frame already in transmission can be
interrupted so an urgent frame goes first, then resumed.

**Stream reservation.** A talker declares its bandwidth and latency needs up
front; the network either guarantees them or refuses the stream.

<Analogy title="Like bus lanes and a timetable">
An ordinary road is fast when empty and unpredictable at rush hour. You cannot
promise an ambulance a journey time.

Add dedicated lanes and a schedule, and certain traffic gets a guarantee
regardless of congestion. Everything else uses the remaining capacity.

TSN is the bus lanes and the timetable. The road is the same Ethernet.
</Analogy>

<Scenario title="Why this is not theoretical">
A consolidated vehicle carries, on one network: four camera feeds, ADAS sensor
data, cluster content, infotainment audio and video, diagnostic traffic, and
vehicle control messages.

Without scheduling, a burst of camera traffic delays a control message by a few
milliseconds. Usually harmless. Occasionally not — and "occasionally" is not
something a safety argument can accommodate.

With TSN, the control message has a reserved slot. The camera traffic uses what
is left. The delay is bounded and the bound is provable, which is what a safety
case actually requires.
</Scenario>

## What this means in practice

**The network is designed, not grown.** Schedules and reservations are computed
offline from a description of all traffic. Adding a stream is a configuration
change, not something that happens spontaneously.

<Plain>
This is worth noticing, because it slightly qualifies the service-oriented
promise. Discovery lets you add a *consumer* freely. Adding a new
high-bandwidth *stream* still touches the network configuration.

In practice programmes reserve headroom for exactly this.
</Plain>

**CAN does not disappear.** Automotive Ethernet is the backbone; CAN remains at
the edges, connecting simple sensors and actuators where its cost and robustness
still win. A gateway translates between them, and that gateway is significant,
ongoing work on every real programme.

```text title="The realistic topology"
   sensors/actuators ──CAN/LIN──> zone controller
                                        │
                                   Automotive
                                    Ethernet
                                    (+ TSN)
                                        │
                                 central compute
```

**Diagnostics change.** Debugging shifts from a CAN analyser to Ethernet packet
capture. Familiar tools work, with automotive-specific dissectors.

```bash title="Looking at the wire"
# CAN, at the edges
candump can0

# Ethernet backbone
tcpdump -i eth0 -w capture.pcap
# then analyse SOME/IP and DDS traffic in Wireshark
```

## What to check on a programme

- **Is TSN actually enabled, or is it Ethernet without timing guarantees?** This
  is a real distinction and the answer is sometimes "not yet".
- **Who owns the network schedule**, and what does adding a stream cost?
- **Where is the CAN boundary**, and who owns the gateway?
- **Is there reserved headroom** for streams added later in the programme?

<Recap>
- CAN's limits are **structural**: 1 Mbit/s, 8-byte payloads, shared bus, no
  addressing.
- Automotive Ethernet runs over **a single twisted pair** with automotive-grade
  connectors and tolerances.
- **Speed was never the problem — predictability was.** TSN adds time sync,
  scheduled traffic, preemption and reservation.
- The network is **designed offline**, so adding a high-bandwidth stream is a
  configuration change even in a service-oriented world.
- **CAN persists at the edges**, and the gateway between old and new is permanent
  work.
</Recap>
